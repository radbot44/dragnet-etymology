## 1. Lexicographical Baseline and the Pre-1889 Negative-Corpus Control

Few Americanisms carry a thicker crust of popular mythology—or a sharper geographic signature—than *hobo*. In the first edition of the *Oxford English Dictionary* (1898), James Murray labeled *hobo, n.* "Of unknown origin" and opened the record with [Josiah Flynt's](https://archive.org/details/dli.bengal.10689.13983) August 1891 *Contemporary Review* essay "The American Tramp" (Vol. 60, printed p. 255, correcting the oft-cited p. 256; CERT-2300-105). For more than half a century after Mitford M. Mathews's *Dictionary of Americanisms* (1951), reference works—including *OED2* (1989), Jonathan E. Lighter's *Historical Dictionary of American Slang*, and [Etymonline](https://www.etymonline.com/word/hobo)—anchored the word's emergence in Pacific Northwest newsprint via the 28 November 1889 [*Ellensburgh Capital*](https://www.dailyrecordnews.com/news/hobo-finds-a-homelibrarians-daughter-makes-discovery/article_42d3e6ec-287d-538b-b765-62f11547dd1b.html) of Ellensburg, Washington: `"The tramp has changed his name, or rather had it changed for him, and now he is a 'hobo'"` (CERT-2300-104). Subsequent scholarship pushed that frontier back in stages: [*Green's Dictionary of Slang*](https://greensdictofslang.com/entry/yz5eshy) cited the 14 September 1888 *Morning Oregonian* (CERT-2300-109); Lighter, Barry Popik, and [Michael Quinion](https://www.worldwidewords.org/qa-hob1.html) flagged an isolated Mexican-American War outlier, `'ho-boying'`, in the 19 August 1848 *New Orleans Daily Picayune* separated by thirty-seven silent years from later usage (CERT-2300-101); and in May 2009 [Stephen Goranson](https://listserv.linguistlist.org/pipermail/ads-l/2009-May/090273.html) identified the 30 November 1885 *St. Paul Daily Globe*, now adopted as the opening witness of [*OED Online*'s March 2023 revision](https://www.oed.com/dictionary/hobo_n) alongside regional baselines for *hobo camp, n.* (29 September 1888 *Winona Daily Republican*), *hobo, v.* (1890 *Spokane Falls Review*), *hoboing, n.* (1891 *Bessemer Journal*), *hoboism, n.* (1892 *Deseret Weekly*), *hobo jungle, n.* (1905 *Daily Utah State Journal*), and *hobohemia, n.* (1908 *Reading Times*; CERT-2300-103).

To test whether *hobo* circulated silently in general Eastern or national tramp print before the mid-1880s, we executed a full-text negative-corpus sweep across six canonical vagrancy, detective, and slang monographs totaling 7,005,840 characters. Allan Pinkerton's [*Strikers, Communists, Tramps and Detectives*](https://archive.org/details/strikerscommunis00pink/page/n37/mode/1up) (New York, 1878; 241 tokens of *tramp/tramps*, CERT-2300-301), U.S. Department of the Interior labor investigator Lee Meriwether's [*A Tramp Trip*](https://archive.org/details/tramptriphowtose00meri/page/n12/mode/1up) (1887; 97 tokens, CERT-2300-302) and [*The Tramp at Home*](https://archive.org/details/trampathome00meri/page/n11/mode/1up) (April 1889, preface December 1888, including Chapter XXV on Washington Territory and Spokane Falls; 85 tokens, CERT-2300-303), John Camden Hotten's [*Slang Dictionary*](https://archive.org/details/cu31924086360488/page/n209/mode/1up) (1874, CERT-2300-304), John S. Farmer's [*Americanisms — Old & New*](https://archive.org/details/americanismsoldn00farmuoft/page/n324/mode/1up) (1889, CERT-2300-305), and Albert Barrère and Charles G. Leland's [*Dictionary of Slang, Jargon & Cant*](https://archive.org/details/dictionaryofslan00barruoft/page/n494/mode/1up) (Vol. I, 1889, CERT-2300-306) collectively contain **zero** occurrences of *hobo*. Even James Maitland's Chicago-published *American Slang Dictionary* (1891) lacked the word, before Funk & Wagnalls' *Standard Dictionary* (Vol. I, 1893, p. 864) registered `ho'bo [Western U. S.]` ('an idle, shiftless, wandering workman'), followed by *Dialect Notes* (1895) and the *Century Dictionary* Supplement (1904; CERT-2300-310). Equally telling, nine consecutive annual volumes of Eugene V. Debs's Terre Haute-based *Locomotive Firemen's Magazine* (1884–1892) contain zero non-toponymic occurrences of *hobo* before Order of Railway Conductors correspondent "Newt" wrote `"Arizona hobo, insect"` from Bakersfield, California, in the July 1891 [*Railway Conductor*](https://archive.org/details/9101RailwayconductorV08) (Vol. 8, p. 405, dated 8 June 1891; CERT-2300-117). The word was genuinely absent from standard Eastern print prior to its explosion along Western rail divisions between late 1885 and 1889.

## 2. Primary-Source Chronology and Western Rail-Corridor Diffusion (1885–1895)

Inspecting the physical Library of Congress page image of the 30 November 1885 [*St. Paul Daily Globe*](https://www.loc.gov/resource/sn90059522/1885-11-30/ed-1/?sp=9) (printed p. 8, cols. 1–2, located at sequence `sp=9` because sequence `sp=8` is classified p. 7; CERT-2300-001) resolves both the bibliographic offset and the social setting of the earliest verified print witness. Writing under the pseudonym "Rhue Saga" about several months spent inside Hennepin County Jail in Minneapolis during the spring of 1885—at the twin eastern headquarters of the Northern Pacific Railroad (completed to Puget Sound in September 1883) and James J. Hill's St. Paul, Minneapolis & Manitoba Railway (later the Great Northern)—the author devotes two subsections (`THE "HOBO."` and `THIEVES' VOCABULARY.`) to the term:

> Hobo is a call to attract attention, the same as Hello in the average citizen's vernacular. It is pronounced with the long sound of the vowel, o, in both syllables, and is sometimes uttered with the aspirate omitted, as "Obo," and is the shibboleth of the fraternity of bums and crooks. It is now commonly applied by them as a generic term to designate the order. Hence "Hobo," when used in a substantative sense, means tramp or crook, as the case may be. For instance, when one says "That man is a Hobo," he means tramp or crook.

Nine scan-verified attestations from February through November 1888 then map the word's rapid western and trans-Mississippi rail diffusion—pushing verified West Coast and railroad-journal print usage **1 year, 9 months, and 15 days** ahead of the November 1889 Ellensburg benchmark:

By early 1889, every major Northern Pacific, Central Pacific, and Pennsylvania trunk division was carrying the word into local print. On 3 January 1889 the [*St. Paul Daily Globe*](https://www.loc.gov/resource/sn90059522/1889-01-03/ed-1/?sp=1) ran the front-page headline `"HOMICIDAL HOBOES"` (CERT-2300-113). On 26 February 1889—nine months before the *Ellensburgh Capital* editorial—Portland's [*Morning Oregonian*](https://archive.org/details/morning-oregonian-1889-02-26) reprinted a vivid sketch from the *Pasco Headlight* at Pasco, Washington Territory, the exact Northern Pacific division junction where the newly opened Stampede Pass / Cascade line through Yakima and Ellensburg met the Spokane main line: `"speaking of the camp fires of the hoboes: Away out amid the clusters of sage brush and along the railroad tracks"` (CERT-2300-114; echoed forty-eight hours later in southern Oregon). By August 1889, editor L. D. Woodruff at the post-flood PRR and B&O reconstruction camps in Johnstown, Pennsylvania, predicted in the [*Johnstown Weekly Democrat*](https://panewsarchive.psu.edu/lccn/sn86083274/1889-08-23/ed-1/seq-4/) (23 August 1889, CERT-2300-115) that `"Hobo"` would `"creep into our dictionaries,"` while Reno's [*Daily Nevada State Journal*](https://archive.org/details/daily-nevada-state-journal-1889-08-31) (31 August 1889, CERT-2300-116) pitted Truckee–Reno printers against `"the Hobos."`

Institutional prosopography across Western rail divisions confirms that railroaders themselves—not eastern city journalists—incubated and spread the word. In Multnomah County, Oregon, Sheriff Kelly and Jailer James operated an official cellblock known as `"the hobo side"` by 10 March 1890 (CERT-2300-212). Along the Union Pacific main line in September 1890, Knights of Labor District Assembly 82 shopmen in North Platte, Laramie, and Cheyenne saluted North Platte correspondent `"Hobo"` alongside General Foreman Hickey and Superintendent Harvey Middleton in the [*Union Pacific Employes' Magazine*](https://archive.org/details/unionpacificemp07corbgoog/page/n208/mode/1up) (CERT-2300-213). Crucially, on 31 January 1892 the [*Los Angeles Times*](https://archive.org/details/sim_los-angeles-times_the-los-angeles-times_1892-01-31/page/n4/mode/1up) (p. 5, CERT-2300-214) documented an occupational railway sense omitted from *OED Online*: Southern Pacific trainmen around Locomotive Engineer James Gates at the old San Fernando Street depot called their settlement `'"Dogtown" among the "hobo" railroad men'`—using *hobo* for transient, seasonal "boomer" railway employees shifting from division to division. Seven months later, Press Woodruff of the *Spokane Review* described Great Northern grading camps at Crossport, Idaho (`"Beer kegs, bung holes and hobos have quit the grade"`, August 1892 [*Northwest Illustrated Monthly Magazine*](https://archive.org/details/sim_smalleys-magazine_1892-08_10_8/page/n12/mode/1up), CERT-2300-215), while Oregon Short Line conductors William Kirwan and S. D. Clark at Pocatello, Idaho—famed in Western boomer lore as the continental `"hobo"` junction—appear alongside municipal `"floaters"` in the 5 January 1895 [*Los Angeles Times*](https://archive.org/details/sim_los-angeles-times_the-los-angeles-times_1895-01-05/page/n4/mode/1up) (CERT-2300-222), Northern Pacific Conductor W. B. Hale hauled boxcar rider John Bell from Tacoma through Ellensburg, Yakima, and Pasco to Spokane in Milton M. Shaw's [*Nine Thousand Miles on a Pullman Train*](https://archive.org/details/ninethousandmile00shawrich/page/n228/mode/1up) (1898, CERT-2300-223), and a Salt Lake City memoir in [*The Juvenile Instructor*](https://archive.org/details/juvenileinstruct392geor/page/n6/mode/1up) (15 January 1904, CERT-2300-225) recalled an Ogden–Salt Lake brakeman on the Oregon Short Line during the c. 1885–1886 Rock Springs unrest calling a passenger `"a hobo from away east"` years before townspeople knew the word.

## 3. Word-Family Expansion, the 1894 "Hobo Armies," and the Sociological Triad (1891–1923)

Once *hobo* reached trunk-line junctions, morphological derivatives and social compounds multiplied rapidly. In the 29 August 1891 [*Pittsburg Dispatch*](https://archive.org/details/per_pittsburg-dispatch_pittsburg-dispatch_1891-08-29_1) (p. 4, CERT-2300-118), an undercover reporter rode freight cars `'hoboing' my way`, tying *OED*'s 1891 baseline for *hoboing, n.* and preceding *Green's* 1893 verb date by two years. On 21 May 1893, New York's [*The Sun*](https://www.loc.gov/resource/sn83030272/1893-05-21/ed-1/?sp=28) (Sec. 3, p. 8, col. 1, CERT-2300-002) printed `"MIGRATIONS OF THE HOBO"`, simultaneously attesting the verbal idiom `had to 'hobo' it` (followed on 18 February 1897 by past-tense `hoboed it` in Portsmouth, Ohio's [*Daily Times*](https://archive.org/details/portsmouth-daily-times-1897-02-18), CERT-2300-122), the unrecorded apocopated noun `'ho` (`"Onct a 'ho' always a 'ho.'"`), and an explicit 1893 self-definition distinguishing working hoboes from non-working tramps. Six months later, a Tacoma dispatch in the 19 November 1893 [*Fresno Morning Republican*](https://archive.org/details/fresno-bee-1893-11-19/page/n2/mode/1up) (p. 3, CERT-2300-216) dubbed vagrant Mrs. King `"THE 'LADY HOBO'"` before Justice Buckman—five years earlier than *OED*'s *hoboette, n.* (1898).

The decisive national catalyst arrived during the spring depression of 1894, when unemployed Western Industrial Armies commandeered Union Pacific and Northern Pacific freight trains to join Jacob Coxey's march on Washington. Associated Press and Western wires immediately christened them the **`Hobo Army`**: Victoria's [*Daily Colonist*](https://archive.org/details/dailycolonist18940429uvic/page/n7/mode/1up) headlined `"THE HOBO ARMY"` over the 28 April 1894 Portland Union Pacific train seizure at Troutdale (29 April 1894, CERT-2300-217); the [*Chicago Daily Tribune*](https://archive.org/details/per_chicago-daily-tribune_the-chicago-daily-tribun_1894-05-01_53_121/page/n1/mode/1up) tracked Galvin's and Hogan's `"army of hobos"` on 1 May 1894 (CERT-2300-218) and printed `hoboism` on 7 May 1894 (CERT-2300-119); General Charles T. Kelly denounced `"professional hoboes"` shadowing his Industrial Army on rafts down the Mississippi in the 27 May 1894 [*St. Louis Post-Dispatch*](https://archive.org/details/per_st-louis-post-dispatch_st-louis-post-dispatch_1894-05-27_45_291/page/n24/mode/1up) (CERT-2300-220); *Northwest Magazine* chronicled William Hogan's Butte-to-Helena Northern Pacific `"old hobo brakebeam riders"` in July 1894 (CERT-2300-221); the [*Los Angeles Herald*](https://www.loc.gov/resource/sn85042461/1894-07-08/ed-1/?sp=4) coined *OED*'s opening token of `hobodom` on 8 July 1894 (CERT-2300-120); and Maine's [*Bangor Daily Whig and Courier*](https://archive.org/details/bangor-daily-whig-and-courier-1894-08-27) reported a raided Buffalo `"Hobo camp"` on 27 August 1894 (CERT-2300-121). Three years later, a Minot, North Dakota dispatch from the Great Northern division point in the 30 June 1897 [*Anaconda Standard*](https://www.loc.gov/resource/sn84036012/1897-06-30/ed-1/?sp=1) (p. 1, col. 5, CERT-2300-003) glossed `"the 'Jungle,' a hobo resort"`—eight years prior to *OED*'s 1905 baseline for *hobo jungle, n.* (later exemplified in the 8 July 1922 [*Oakland Tribune*](https://archive.org/details/oakland-tribune-1922-07-08), CERT-2300-125). Meanwhile, the blend *hobohemia*—anchored by *OED3* in 1908—entered national modernist art and fiction via Stuart Davis's satirical map `"HOBOHEMIA"` in the June 1915 [*Current Opinion*](https://archive.org/details/sim_current-opinion_1915-06_58_6) (Vol. 58, p. 431, CERT-2300-123), twenty-two months ahead of Sinclair Lewis's 7 April 1917 [*Saturday Evening Post*](https://archive.org/details/sim_saturday-evening-post_1917-04-07_189_41) story `"Hobohemia"` (CERT-2300-124).

Our primary scans also reconstruct the exact thirty-year genealogy of the sociological triad (`hobo` vs. `tramp` vs. `bum`) codified in Nels Anderson's University of Chicago monograph [*The Hobo: The Sociology of the Homeless Man*](https://archive.org/details/hobosociologyofh00ande/page/n110/mode/1up) (May 1923, p. 87, CERT-2300-230). After the 21 May 1893 *New York Sun* contrast between the non-working tramp and the occasionally working hobo (CERT-2300-002) and E. Lamar Bailey's October 1898 [*Forum*](https://archive.org/details/sim_forum-and-century_1898-10_26_2/page/n89/mode/1up) dictum (`"A tramp is not a hobo; a hobo is not a tramp; a vagrant is neither"`, CERT-2300-224), Spokane's Industrial Workers of the World ("Wobblies") elevated the freight-riding *hobo* into the backbone of Western timber and harvest unionism—celebrated on a rare c. September 1908 Spokane pocket song card printing `"Hallelujah, I'm a bum!"` alongside `"For I'm a hobo!"` ([*I.W.W. Songs*](https://archive.org/details/industrialworker00unse/page/n2/mode/1up), CERT-2300-227) and in Spokane's [*Industrial Worker*](https://archive.org/details/v1n36-nov-24-1909-IW/page/n1/mode/1up) during the November 1909 Free-Speech Fight (`"This 'hobo' union..."`, CERT-2300-228). Fifteen years before Anderson, **Dr. Ben L. Reitman** printed the exact behavioral triad in Appendix B of Edmond Kelly's [*The Elimination of the Tramp*](https://archive.org/details/eliminationoftra00kelluoft/page/n133/mode/1up) (February 1908, pp. 103–104, CERT-2300-226: `1. TRAMP — a. Dreams and wanders... 2. HOBO — a. Works and wanders. b. Hoboland—farms, ice-houses, section houses, mines, etc. ... 3. BUM — a. Drinks and wanders`), and six years before Anderson, **Rev. Irwin St. John Tucker** of Chicago's International Hobo College formulated the crisp mobility-by-labor matrix in a 24 February 1917 wire published in the [*Oakland Tribune*](https://archive.org/details/oakland-tribune-1917-02-25/page/n43/mode/1up) (25 February 1917, CERT-2300-229: `"Hobo—A migratory worker. Tramp—A migratory non-worker. Bum—A stationary non-worker"`).

## 4. Evaluating Origin Hypotheses (H1–H3)

Testing the three serious philological proposals against our 1885–1894 primary corpus sharply separates an empirically attested rail-corridor mechanism from weaker agricultural and British dialectal conjectures:

1. **H1 — Western Rail-Yard Greeting and Freight-Car Shibboleth (`"Ho, boy!"` / `"Ho! Bo!"` -> `hoboy` / `hobo`, Supporting Pathway):** Every contemporary witness closest in time and place to the word's emergence describes *hobo* first as an oral **attention call or recognition hail** along railroad lines. In November 1885, "Rhue Saga" in St. Paul defined `"Hobo"` (variant `"Obo"`) explicitly as `"a call to attract attention, the same as Hello"` that had `"now"` been nominalized as `"a generic term to designate the order"` (CERT-2300-001). In September 1888, Portland Police Captain Cardwell independently reported that tramps walking beside closed freight cars repeated `"hobo"` as `"a sort of password"` to have the boxcar door slid open (CERT-2300-109)—testimony echoed verbatim in Astoria (CERT-2300-110) and corroborated in May 1894 by an experienced road veteran interviewed by the *Worcester Gazette* ([*Newport Daily News*](https://archive.org/details/newport-daily-news-1894-05-15/page/n3/mode/1up), 15 May 1894, CERT-2300-219). In August 1889, Johnstown editor L. D. Woodruff derived `"Hobo"` from shouted `"Hello ! boy"` (CERT-2300-115); in May 1893, New York hoboes explained `"he calls ye 'Ho' an' ye calls him 'Bo'"` (CERT-2300-002); and in [*Dialect Notes*](https://archive.org/details/bwb_Y0-BYK-565_5/page/n97/mode/1up) (Vol. V, 1920, pp. 86–87, CERT-2300-307), H. R. Jeffrey documented from a family railwayman working on the **Oregon Short Line in the 1880s** that railway mail carriers shouted `"Ho, boy!"` when handing off mail pouches, became known as `"hoboys"`, and passed the clipped trochee `"hobo"` (`-y` dropped after `/oʊ/`, reinforced by vowel harmony `/hoʊ boʊ/`) to track walkers and freight riders—paralleled by pre-1889 night-duty hails `"Ho! boys! Ho! boys!"` (*Democratic Watchman*, 28 September 1877, CERT-2300-307). Phonologically and chronologically, Admiral W. H. Smyth's [*Sailor's Word-Book*](https://archive.org/details/smythsailorswordbook/page/n158/mode/1up) (1867, p. 111, CERT-2300-308) proves that clipped vocative `BO` (`< boy`) already circulated in maritime English before 1870, whereas inland tramp `'bo` ('a hobo') is an aphetic 1890s clipping *from* `hobo`, consistently printed with an initial apostrophe in Josiah Flynt's [*Tramping with Tramps*](https://archive.org/details/trampingwithtra00willgoog/page/n393/mode/1up) (1899, pp. 388, 392, 394) and paired with `bum < bummer` by H. L. Mencken in [*The American Language*](https://archive.org/details/americanlanguage00mencuoft/page/n176/mode/1up) (1919, p. 161, CERT-2300-309; cf. [Anatoly Liberman's 2008 synthesis](https://blog.oup.com/2008/11/hobo/)).
2. **H2 — Migratory Agricultural `"hoe-boy"` (Weakened / Unlikely Pathway):** Popularized in March 1922 by Hobo College attorney Nicholas Klein and quoted sceptically in Nels Anderson's [*The Hobo*](https://archive.org/details/hobosociologyofh00ande_0/page/n109/mode/1up) (1923, pp. 88–89, CERT-2300-312), the claim that Western migratory farmhands were called `"hoe-boys"` fails corpus verification. Across twenty-four 19th-century American farm and plantation manuals (`hoe-hand` and `hoe-gang` standard) and all pre-1889 Pacific Northwest, Idaho, and Montana newspapers, *hoe-boy* never designates a migratory harvest worker; pre-1889 American print yields only 1818 Georgia Cherokee-mission schoolboys (*The Reflector*, 4 August 1818) and an 1885 *hautboy* pun (*The Sunny South*, 15 August 1885; CERT-2300-311).
3. **H3 — Northern English Dialect `hawbuck` / `hawbaw` (Weakened / Unlikely Pathway):** Advanced tentatively by Robert K. Barnhart (1988), dialectal British `hawbuck` ('clumsy country youth, lout'), verified in Cumbrian poet John Stagg's [*Miscellaneous Poems*](https://archive.org/details/miscellaneouspoe00stag/page/n155/mode/1up) (1808, p. 146) and J. O. Halliwell's *Dictionary of Archaic and Provincial Words* (1852, p. 438, CERT-2300-313) as well as Joseph Wright's [*English Dialect Dictionary*](https://archive.org/details/englishdialectdi03wrig/page/n107/mode/1up) (Vol. III, 1902, p. 96, alongside Yorkshire `hawbaw`, CERT-2300-314), faces severe phonological and geographic obstacles: unexplained loss of final velar `-k`, unmotivated vowel raising `/ɔː...ʌ/ -> /oʊ...oʊ/`, absence from Western rail camps, and an attested 1865 New Jersey sense meaning `'a tomboy'` (*Dialect Notes*, 1896, p. 418, CERT-2300-314).

## 5. Folk-Etymology Refutations and Archival Trap Hygiene

Fifteen rejected leads document both popular folk etymologies and the three classes of digital-archive noise that corrupt naive pre-1889 searches:

- **Falsified Folk Etymologies (H4):** (1) **Hoboken, New Jersey** (`Hoboken` / `"Hobokenite"`): Already refuted by a road hobo in May 1894 (CERT-2300-219), the Hoboken terminal myth inverts geography by 2,500 miles—zero pre-1889 New Jersey or New York uses exist while Sylva Clapin's *New Dictionary of Americanisms* (1902, p. 228) marks *hobo* Western, the Lackawanna Terminal dates to 1907, and George Philip Krapp's [*English Language in America*](https://archive.org/details/englishlanguagei01krap_0/page/n195/mode/1up) (1925, Vol. I, p. 176) explains Hoboken's comic vaudeville aura from its Lenape name *Hobocan-hackingh* ('land of the tobacco pipe'; CERT-2300-315). (2) **Latin *homo bonus* ("good man"):** Traced in primary newsprint to self-styled "King of the Hoboes" Jeff Davis at New York's Hotel de Gink in the 12 January 1915 Harrisburg [*Star-Independent*](https://panewsarchive.psu.edu/lccn/sn86081330/1915-01-12/ed-1/seq-4/) (`"derived from two Latin words—homo, meaning man, and bonus, meaning good"`, CERT-2300-316), five months earlier than *Green's* June 1915 citation. (3) **Civil War `"Homeward Bound"` (`Ho. Bo.`):** Anachronistic pre-WWI acronymic back-projection completely absent from 1865–1888 veteran print; Pinkerton (1878, p. 48) explicitly discusses demobilized Civil War soldier-wanderers under `"tramps"` alone, while `"Homeward Bound"` appears only in unrelated 1915 religious newsprint (CERT-2300-317).
- **Archival Traps (Traps A–C):** Five **Trap A** serial and microfilm misdates expose Chronicling America's `1889-01-02` *Butte Semi-Weekly Miner* as **2 January 1890** via adjacent `Dec. 24, 1889` / `State of Montana` official notices (CERT-2300-112), two Google Books *Railway Conductor* bound volumes stamped `"1889"` as **October 1900** (Vol. 17, CERT-2300-232) and **May 1912** (Vol. 29, CERT-2300-126), *Scribner's Magazine* stamped `"1887"` as **1898** (Vol. 23, Wyckoff's *The Workers*, CERT-2300-318), and *The Century Magazine* stamped `"1881"` as **October 1916** (Vol. 92, CERT-2300-319). Five **Trap B** dirty-OCR ghosts unmask two treacherous 1885 Northern Pacific phantoms where smudged `"St. Paul Globe"` was read by OCR as `"St. Paul (Hobo"` (3 January 1885 *St. Paul Daily Globe*, CERT-2300-127; 22 May 1885 *Brainerd Dispatch*, CERT-2300-231), `"Carriage Robes"` as `"Carriage Hobos"` (2 January 1885 *National Republican*, CERT-2300-128; 22 April 1858 *American Volunteer*, CERT-2300-320), `"Robo"` as `"Hobo"` (1 April 1885 *Somerset Herald*, CERT-2300-130), and `"HOME"` / `"MORGAN'S"` as `"HOBOI"` / `"HOBO"` (1865 & 1873 Pennsylvania papers, CERT-2300-320). Finally, three **Trap C** homograph controls isolate early English `hoboy` / `hautboy` ('oboe') and New York `"Ho-Boy"` ('night-soil scavenger', Bartlett 1859/1884, CERT-2300-321; Farmer 1889, CERT-2300-305), Meiji Japanese `hobo` ('nearly') and `hōbō` ('everywhere; Buddhist dharma treasure', Imbrie 1880, CERT-2300-129; Hepburn 1888, CERT-2300-322), and Spanish *bobo* ('dunce', Velázquez 1872, CERT-2300-322).
