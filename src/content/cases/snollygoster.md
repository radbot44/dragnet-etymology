## 1. What the Sources Establish and Where Prior Work Stands

In the unrevised 1933 *Supplement* entry maintained on [Oxford English Dictionary Online (`snollygoster, n.`)](https://www.oed.com/dictionary/snollygoster_n), **snollygoster** jumps across forty-nine years—from an April 7, 1846 frontier boast in the Frankfort, Kentucky *Commonwealth* (`Snolly Goster`, supplied by [Hans Sperber in *American Speech*, 1953](https://www.jstor.org/stable/453172)) to the October 28, 1895 *Columbus Evening Dispatch* definition (`"monumental talknophical assumnacy"`)—while tentatively comparing **snallygaster**, the winged monster of Frederick County, Maryland lore.

Substantial pieces of the earlier record have already been mapped in lexicographical notes and mailing-list dispatches:

- **1845–1846 tall-talk reprints**: [Online Etymology Dictionary](https://www.etymonline.com/word/snollygoster) cited *Scientific American* (Dec. 18, 1845, `CERT-3200-105`), and Jonathan Lighter reported a January 1, 1846 Detroit *Democratic Free Press* reprint on [ADS-L (Mar. 2013)](https://listserv.linguistlist.org/pipermail/ads-l/2013-March/126244.html) and an October 9, 1845 Woodstock, Virginia *Sentinel of the Valley* reprint on [ADS-L (Jan. 6, 2024)](https://listserv.linguistlist.org/pipermail/ads-l/2024-January/164683.html) (`CERT-3200-102`). Crucially, Ben Zimmer had already pushed the public report of the same sketch back into **September 1845** on [ADS-L (Feb. 7, 2017)](https://listserv.linguistlist.org/pipermail/ads-l/2017-February/146378.html) (`CERT-3200-100`), identifying three reprints of *"That Tarnal Railroad, as spoken at the Fulton Institute"* credited to the *Cincinnati Commercial*: Philadelphia's *Dollar Newspaper* (Sept. 10, 1845, p. 1, col. 7), Maryland's *Easton Star* (Sept. 23, 1845, p. 1, col. 1), and Indianapolis's *The Locomotive* (Sept. 27, 1845, p. 1, col. 2).
- **Mid-century political uses**: Both [Lighter's 2024 ADS-L post](https://listserv.linguistlist.org/pipermail/ads-l/2024-January/164683.html) and [Merriam-Webster's `Snollygoster vs. Snallygaster` note](https://www.merriam-webster.com/grammar/snollygoster-or-snallygaster-usage) (`CERT-3200-414`) quote the October 25, 1866 *White Cloud Kansas Chief* attack on State Senate nominee `"R. Cole Foster, the Snolly-goster"` (`CERT-3200-206`), while [Merriam-Webster's Wordplay essay](https://www.merriam-webster.com/wordplay/whats-a-snollygoster) noted 1850s Southern political currency and February 1893 reports of Georgia lecturer H. W. J. Ham.
- **The monster question**: [Merriam-Webster](https://www.merriam-webster.com/grammar/snollygoster-or-snallygaster-usage) pointed out that *snallygaster* enters print decades after *snollygoster* and discussed Pennsylvania German *schnelle geeschter* ('quick spirits'), whereas folklorist [Jack Daly (*Contemporary Legend* 4.04, 2026, pp. 160–162)](https://scholarworks.iu.edu/journals/index.php/cl/article/download/41748/44361) (building on Trevor J. Blank and David J. Puglia, *Maryland Legends*, 2014, pp. 88, 91) proposed influence in the opposite direction—that political *snollygoster* helped shape *Snallygaster*.

Checking sixty primary and secondary records (`CERT-3200-100` through `CERT-3200-414`, backed by fifty-five pre-1931 composite WebP scans) separates what the dated ink establishes from where inferential gaps remain.

## 2. The 1845 Fulton Institute Reprints and 1846–1853 Spelling Shifts

### 2.1 Reported September 1845 Reprints vs. Scan-Verified October 9, 1845 Reprints

Pre-1845 dirty-OCR candidates dissolve on inspection into catalog misdates (`CERT-3200-106`, where Internet Archive tags [*Crockett's 1846 Almanac*](https://archive.org/details/crockettalmanacc1846croc/page/n29/mode/1up) as `1841`) or broken type (`CERT-3200-115`, where 1830 Savannah OCR `Snolli.-rn Ili-view` prints `Southern Review` in [*The Georgian*](https://gahistoricnewspapers.galileo.usg.edu/lccn/sn83016087/1830-11-16/ed-1/seq-2/)). As [Ben Zimmer documented in 2017](https://listserv.linguistlist.org/pipermail/ads-l/2017-February/146378.html) (`CERT-3200-100`), the earliest known printings of *"That Tarnal Railroad, as spoken at the Fulton Institute"* are three September 1845 reprints crediting the *Cincinnati Commercial*, beginning **September 10, 1845** in Philadelphia's *Dollar Newspaper* (p. 1, col. 7, cited to GenealogyBank), followed by the *Easton Star* (Sept. 23, p. 1, col. 1, GenealogyBank) and Indianapolis's *The Locomotive* (Sept. 27, p. 1, col. 2, whose [Hoosier State Chronicles link](https://newspapers.library.in.gov/cgi-bin/indiana?a=d&d=LO18450927.1.1) currently returns HTTP 403). Neither the *Cincinnati Daily Commercial* nor the *Dollar Weekly Commercial* is digitized for 1845, so we verified Zimmer's public report rather than the September 1845 ink and avoid guessing an exact debut date for the Fulton Institute performance.

Our earliest directly inspected scans are **two scan-verified reprints** dated **Thursday, October 9, 1845**—Woodstock, Virginia's [*Sentinel of the Valley* (p. 4, cols. 1–2)](https://www.loc.gov/resource/sn95079484/1845-10-09/ed-1/?sp=4) (`CERT-3200-102`, previously reported by [Lighter in 2024](https://listserv.linguistlist.org/pipermail/ads-l/2024-January/164683.html)) and southern Maryland's [*Port Tobacco Times, and Charles County Advertiser* (p. 4, col. 1)](https://www.loc.gov/resource/sn89060060/1845-10-09/ed-1/?sp=4) (`CERT-3200-103`):

> Now, here I am, a rale self-propelling double-revolving locomotive Snolly Goster, ready to attack anything but a combination of Thunder-lightning-smoke, railroad-iron and hot water. I’d like for some of your gals to give me a chance to play hop scotch among your hearts, and if I don’t play the swill barrel game by walking right straight through your tarnal prejudices into your young affections, then help me Jezey.—Cin. Com.

Because both October 9 papers credit the *Cincinnati Commercial*, they corroborate exchange circulation across Virginia and Maryland rather than independent coinages. Two months later, [*The Scientific American* (Dec. 18, 1845, Vol. 1, No. 14, p. 1, col. 4)](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Scientific_American_-_Series_1_-_Volume_001_-_Issue_14.pdf) (`CERT-3200-105`, preceded on Nov. 7, 1845 by Vermont's [*Burlington Free Press*](https://www.loc.gov/resource/sn84023127/1845-11-07/ed-1/?sp=1), `CERT-3200-104`) provides an open scan of the subtitle **`That Tarnal Railroad. (AS SPOKEN AT THE FULTON INSTITUTE.)`** while trimming the closing courtship sentence.

## 3. Political Context, Satire, and the Office-Seeking Sense, 1855–1953

### 3.1 From the 1855 Lodge Parody and 1864 Song to the 1866 Foster Insult

Between the 1840s railroad sketch and the 1890s campaign definitions, *snollygoster* entered partisan print in stages that should not be conflated with a full lexical definition. After the Know-Nothing split of June 1855, Major Beatty of Bath County, Kentucky organized a mock fraternal order reported in Athens, Georgia's [*The Southern Banner* (June 28, 1855, p. 3, col. 4)](https://gahistoricnewspapers.galileo.usg.edu/lccn/sn82014069/1855-06-28/ed-1/seq-3/) (`CERT-3200-201`) and credited to the Lexington *Kentucky Statesman* in Illinois's [*Canton Weekly Register* (Aug. 2, 1855, p. 1, col. 4)](https://www.loc.gov/resource/sn84038367/1855-08-02/ed-1/?sp=1) (`CERT-3200-202`):

> The Immortal Snollygosters. This is the name of a new order lately organized in Kentucky, at ‘the head and front’ of which is Major Beatty, of Bath county, which promises to supersede the dark lantern sink holes. ... First Plank.—Any white man has as good a right to vote as a negro if he behaves himself.

During the Civil War, Unionists mocked Confederate General Felix K. Zollicoffer as `Snolligoster` ([*Pennsylvania Daily Telegraph*, Nov. 1, 1861, p. 2, col. 4](https://panewsarchive.psu.edu/lccn/sn84035968/1861-11-01/ed-1/seq-2/), `CERT-3200-203`), Daniel Decatur Emmett used `de snolly-gosters` in [*The Black Brigade* (1863, Plate 5560, p. 5)](https://www.loc.gov/resource/lprbscsm.scsm0043/) (`CERT-3200-204`), and J. B. Murphy's St. Louis song [*Snolly-goster Ebenezer* (1864, pp. 3, 5)](https://www.loc.gov/resource/lprbscsm.scsm0120/) (`CERT-3200-205`) combined a first-stanza brag (`"I’m a reg’-lar Snol-ly gos-ter"`) with second-stanza satire against `"hungry politicians, dat would like to get in office"` and `"honeyswoglers"`. Neither the 1855 lodge parody nor the 1864 song defines *snollygoster*.

In the [*White Cloud Kansas Chief* (Oct. 25, 1866, p. 2, col. 2)](https://www.loc.gov/resource/sn82015486/1866-10-25/ed-1/?sp=2) (`CERT-3200-206`), already quoted by both [Jonathan Lighter (2024)](https://listserv.linguistlist.org/pipermail/ads-l/2024-January/164683.html) and [Merriam-Webster](https://www.merriam-webster.com/grammar/snollygoster-or-snallygaster-usage), editor Sol Miller applied the singular noun to a legislative nominee:

> The Democrats of Leavenworth have nominated a full County ticket. We see that “R. Cole Foster, the Snolly-goster,” is one of the nominees for the State Senate. He had better stay at home; there will be no person at the Capital, next Winter, to properly take care of his morals.

The second sentence strengthens the personal insult against a political candidate, though—like the Sept. 21, 1848 *National Eagle* `'natural born ASS'` gloss noted by [Lighter (2024)](https://listserv.linguistlist.org/pipermail/ads-l/2024-January/164683.html)—it still stops short of defining *snollygoster* specifically as a chronic office-seeker.

### 3.2 Outsized Specimens (`1873–1879`), H. W. J. Ham's `1892` Definition, and Truman (`1952`)

Alongside personal insults, *snollygoster* developed an inanimate and zoological 'crusher / whopper' use: an editorial blast in [*The Huntingdon Journal* (Feb. 26, 1873, p. 2, col. 2)](https://panewsarchive.psu.edu/lccn/sn83032115/1873-02-26/ed-1/seq-2/) (`CERT-3200-207`), a Nevada range notice in Carson City's [*Morning Appeal* (Oct. 4, 1877, p. 3, col. 3)](https://www.loc.gov/resource/sn86076999/1877-10-04/ed-1/?sp=3) (`CERT-3200-208`), James A. Henshall's forty-pound Florida redfish in [*Forest and Stream* (Oct. 9, 1879, \[p. 703\], col. 3)](https://archive.org/details/sim_forest-and-stream-a-journal-of-outdoor-life_1879-10-09_13_10/page/n0/mode/1up) (`CERT-3200-401`, five years before Henshall's 1884 book), and the Ozark intensive `super-snolly-gonchus` in [*Dialect Notes* Vol. III, Part I (1905, p. 97)](https://archive.org/details/dialectnotes03newhuoft/page/97/mode/1up) (`CERT-3200-214`).

The clearer pre-1895 evidence for the specific **office-seeking** sense arrives in autumn 1892 with Gainesville, Georgia stump speaker **Hon. Henry W. J. Ham** (1851–1907). Following campaign reports in the [*New York Sun* (Sept. 29, 1892, p. 6, col. 3)](https://www.loc.gov/resource/sn83030272/1892-09-29/ed-1/?sp=6) (`CERT-3200-209`) and Savannah's [*The Morning News* (Oct. 4, 1892, p. 4, col. 1)](https://gahistoricnewspapers.galileo.usg.edu/lccn/sn86063034/1892-10-04/ed-1/seq-4/) (`CERT-3200-210`), Henderson, North Carolina's [*The Gold Leaf* (Oct. 27, 1892, p. 2, col. 3)](https://www.loc.gov/resource/sn91068402/1892-10-27/ed-1/?sp=2) (`CERT-3200-211`) explicitly defined the political type four months before [Merriam-Webster's](https://www.merriam-webster.com/wordplay/whats-a-snollygoster) February 1893 benchmark and three years before OED's October 1895 quotation:

> “SNOLLYGOSTER” is a word coined by Ham, the orator from the Georgia mountains. A snollygoster he defined as a “fellow with an unquenchable thirst for office with neither the power to get it nor capacity to fill it.” There are several specimens of the “snollygoster” type among the Third party in Vance connty [sic].

Ham's own January 1893 story claiming an 1848 Rabun County coinage ([*The Herald and Advertiser*, Jan. 27, 1893](https://gahistoricnewspapers.galileo.usg.edu/lccn/sn89053456/1893-01-27/ed-1/seq-1/), `CERT-3200-114`; cf. [*The Atchison Blade*, Mar. 25, 1893](https://www.loc.gov/resource/sn83040110/1893-03-25/ed-1/?sp=4), `CERT-3200-212`, and the rebuttal in [*The Utahnian*, June 20, 1896](https://archive.org/details/sim_utahnian_1896-06-20_1_1/page/n2/mode/1up), `CERT-3200-412`) is contradicted by the 1845–1846 newspapers, while Des Moines's [*Iowa State Bystander* (Nov. 15, 1895, p. 2, col. 1)](https://www.loc.gov/resource/sn83025186/1895-11-15/ed-1/?sp=2) (`CERT-3200-213`) identifies the unnamed `"Georgia editor"` behind OED's 1895 `"monumental, talknophical assumnacy"` quotation as **`Col. Hamm, editor of the Georgia Cracker`**. President Harry S. Truman's whistlestop remark on Republican `"snollygosters"` at Parkersburg, West Virginia is dated to **September 2, 1952, 12:45 p.m.** in the [Truman Library Public Papers](https://www.trumanlibrary.gov/library/public-papers/242/rear-platform-remarks-ohio-and-west-virginia) (`CERT-3200-215`; cf. his uncorroborated Nov. 1953 gloss in [*The Evening Star*, Nov. 11, 1953](https://www.loc.gov/resource/sn83045462/1953-11-11/ed-1/?sp=1), `CERT-3200-216`).

## 4. Dialectal Ingredients and the Chronological Gap Before Snallygaster

### 4.1 Dialectal `goster` as a Plausible Ingredient

British provincial glossaries document a northern and Midland verb and noun **`goster` / `gauster`** ('boisterous bragging; to boast, bully, hector, domineer', `<` Middle English *galstren*): [John Ray (1674, p. 67)](https://archive.org/details/bim_early-english-books-1641-1700_a-collection-of-english-_ray-john_1674/page/n80/mode/1up) (`To Goyſter`, `CERT-3200-301`), [Francis Grose (1787)](https://archive.org/details/aprovincialglos00unkngoog/page/n157/mode/1up) (`GAUSTER / GOYSTER`, `CERT-3200-302`), [William Carr (1828, Vol. I, p. 177)](https://archive.org/details/dialectcravenin00carrgoog/page/n193/mode/1up) (`GAUSTERING, Imperious, boasting`, `CERT-3200-303`), [Joseph Hunter (1829, p. 45)](https://archive.org/details/bwb_T2-EYP-749/page/n76/mode/1up) (`GOSTER, to boast, to bully, to hector`, `CERT-3200-304`), [J. O. Halliwell (1846)](https://archive.org/details/halliwellarchaicandprovincialwords01/page/n418/mode/1up) (`CERT-3200-305`), and [Samuel Bamford (1854, p. 181)](https://archive.org/details/dialectofsouthla00bobb/page/n208/mode/1up) (`CERT-3200-306`). In the United States, Georgetown, Ohio's [*Democratic Standard* (Nov. 16, 1841, p. 1, col. 5)](https://www.loc.gov/resource/sn83035312/1841-11-16/ed-1/?sp=1) (`CERT-3200-101`) reprinted `"What do you mane by all this goster?"` from the *New Orleans Picayune* in stylized stage-Irish dialogue comparing bluster to `"maykin’ motions in Washington"` as a member of Congress, and Francis Bazley Lee recorded living New Jersey `goster: to domineer. gosterer: one who boasts or brags` in [*Dialect Notes* Vol. I, Part VII (1894, p. 331)](https://archive.org/details/dialectnotes01newhuoft/page/n340/mode/1up) (`CERT-3200-307`; synthesized in [Wright's *English Dialect Dictionary* Vol. II, 1900, pp. 579–580](https://archive.org/details/englishdialectdi02wrig/page/n590/mode/1up), `CERT-3200-308`).

Together with pejorative `sn-` formations or Scots/Ulster `snool ~ snoll` ([Jamieson 1808, Vol. II, sig. 4X2r](https://archive.org/details/etymologicaldict02jami/page/n441/mode/1up), `CERT-3200-309`; [Wright *EDD* Vol. V, 1905, pp. 590, 592](https://archive.org/details/englishdialectdi05wrig/page/n603/mode/1up), `CERT-3200-310`) and Jacksonian four-syllable comic compounds ([Bartlett 1848 & 1859](https://archive.org/details/dictionaryofamer00bart/page/n99/mode/1up); [Farmer 1889](https://archive.org/details/americanismsoldn00farmuoft/page/n330/mode/1up); [Thornton 1912](https://archive.org/details/b3136066x_0001/page/n472/mode/1up); `CERT-3200-311`–`CERT-3200-314`), dialectal *goster* is a **plausible ingredient**—not an established derivation. The 1841 Ohio newspaper item is a New Orleans stage-dialect reprint rather than evidence of everyday Cincinnati speech, and no contemporary source explains how the Fulton Institute writer coined `Snolly Goster`.

### 4.2 Why Chronology Weakens the Monster-Origin Story Without Proving the Reverse

The sixty-four-year gap between 1845 `Snolly Goster` and the February 1909 *Middletown Valley Register* stories introducing Maryland's winged **Snallygaster** ([*Boundary Stones*, Dec. 4, 2024](https://boundarystones.weta.org/2024/12/04/maryland-snallygaster-devil-racist-politics)) weakens the traditional claim that *snollygoster* arose as an alteration of *snallygaster*, while leaving the origin of the 1909 monster name itself open:

- **What pre-1909 `-goster` and `-gaster` texts show—and what they do not**: `'AN INDIANA HYMN.'` in [*Judge* (Feb. 15, 1890, p. 313, col. 2, crediting *Chicago Mail*)](https://archive.org/details/sim_judge_1890-02-15_17_435/page/n14/mode/1up) (`CERT-3200-403`; an earlier Jan. 25, 1890 *Buffalo Morning Express* printing is cited by [Merriam-Webster](https://www.merriam-webster.com/grammar/snollygoster-or-snallygaster-usage)) warns wife-beaters about `"the snolly-goster men"`, but its refrain explicitly identifies them as human Indiana **`White Caps`** vigilantes (`"An' the White Caps'll git you"`), not supernatural creatures. Bellefonte, Pennsylvania's [*The Centre Democrat* (Nov. 29, 1894, p. 8, col. 3)](https://panewsarchive.psu.edu/lccn/sn84009409/1894-11-29/ed-1/seq-8/) (`CERT-3200-402`) genuinely prints unrounded `-gaster` in `"The Snollygaster in Pollytics"` (cf. Oct. 11, 1900 *Brooklyn Daily Eagle* `"The Snallygaster in Politics"` in [Merriam-Webster](https://www.merriam-webster.com/grammar/snollygoster-or-snallygaster-usage)), and Walt McDougall's [*The Rambillicus Book* (July 1903, p. 140)](https://archive.org/details/rambillicusbookw00mcdo/page/140/mode/1up) (`CERT-3200-404`; cf. [scan plate](https://dragnet.tch.one/scans/snollygoster/CERT-3200-404.webp) and William T. Cox's 1910 southern swamp `snoligoster` in [*Fearsome Creatures of the Lumberwoods*, p. 15](https://archive.org/details/fearsomecreatur00coxgoog/page/n20/mode/1up), `CERT-3200-405`) genuinely uses `The Ticklish Snollygaster` for a fictional beast. Yet neither the 1894 lecture notice nor McDougall's 1903 book ties the word to the 1909 *Middletown Valley Register* writers. Folklorist [Jack Daly (*Contemporary Legend* 4.04, 2026, pp. 160–162)](https://scholarworks.iu.edu/journals/index.php/cl/article/download/41748/44361) (citing Blank and Puglia 2014, pp. 88, 91) earlier proposed that political *snollygoster* influenced *Snallygaster* alongside German lore, which remains a plausible hypothesis rather than proof.
- **Hedged 1940 German explanation and Pennsylvania German lexicon checks**: On page 7 of the August 1940 WPA [*Maryland: A Guide to the Old Line State*](https://archive.org/details/marylandaguideto012121mbp/page/n38/mode/1up) (`CERT-3200-413`; cf. p. 348 `"Schnelle geist"` and [Leo Spitzer's 1952 note in *American Speech*](https://www.jstor.org/stable/453495)), the guide explicitly hedges its German explanation—`"The name, those who have investigated the legend believe, may well have been a corruption of the German schnellegeister, meaning 'quick spirit'"`—rather than claiming its authors invented it. Four 1872–1924 Pennsylvania German reference works—[S. S. Haldeman (1872, p. 12)](https://archive.org/details/pennsylvaniadutc00haldiala/page/12/mode/1up) (`CERT-3200-406`), [Lee L. Grumbine (1887, p. xii)](https://archive.org/details/proceedin172418851892amer/page/n89/mode/1up) (`CERT-3200-408`), [A. R. Horne (1896, p. 157)](https://archive.org/details/pennsylvaniager00horngoog/page/n163/mode/1up) (`CERT-3200-407`), and [Marcus Bachman Lambert's 16,438-entry dictionary (1924, p. 137)](https://archive.org/details/dictionaryofnone00lamb/page/137/mode/1up) (`CERT-3200-409`)—record zero compound entries for `*schnellegeischt`, `*schnellegeeschter`, `*schnellegeister`, or `*snallygaster` (and [Meyer 1880, p. 72](https://archive.org/details/derrichtigeberl01meyegoog/page/n89/mode/1up), `CERT-3200-410`, and [Kluge 1899, p. 350](https://archive.org/details/etymologisches00klug/page/n381/mode/1up), `CERT-3200-411`, omit twentieth-century Berlin slang *schnieke*). At the same time, Haldeman writes `"perhaps universally"` when noting `sp, st > schp, scht` (`'geescht'` for *gehest*), and [Merriam-Webster](https://www.merriam-webster.com/grammar/snollygoster-or-snallygaster-usage) discusses Pennsylvania German *schnelle geeschter*, which already contains that palatalized `/ʃt/` sound—so dictionary absence does not by itself rule out informal oral adaptation into the 1909 Maryland monster tales.

## 5. Remaining Open Questions

Before reusing the working OED slips externally, two archival checks remain priority tasks: inspecting original page images of the three September 1845 reprints reported by [Ben Zimmer (2017)](https://listserv.linguistlist.org/pipermail/ads-l/2017-February/146378.html) alongside any surviving 1845 *Cincinnati Commercial* file, and locating H. W. J. Ham's upstream autumn 1892 Georgia print source behind the October 27, 1892 *Gold Leaf* definition (`CERT-3200-211`). Direct microfilm inspection of the February–March 1909 *Middletown Valley Register* issues is likewise needed to test whether the 1909 Maryland writers alluded to political *snollygoster* or to German spirit lore.
