## 1. Prior Scholarship and Lexicographical Baseline

In the [*Oxford English Dictionary* (`jamboree, n.`)](https://www.oed.com/dictionary/jamboree_n) (CERT-2500-903), the entry remains an unrevised carryover from James A. H. Murray's October 1900 First Edition fascicle (*A New English Dictionary on Historical Principles*, Vol. V, [p. 549](https://archive.org/details/newenglishdict05murruoft/page/548/mode/2up), CERT-2500-901) augmented by 20th-century draft additions. When Murray published the original entry in 1900, his earliest citation was Maximilian Schele De Vere's *Americanisms; The English of the New World* ([1872, p. 488](https://archive.org/details/americanismsengl00scheuoft/page/488/mode/2up), CERT-2500-902), which recorded *Jamboree* only as an unlocalized Americanism for a noisy frolic, followed five years later by John Russell Bartlett's fourth edition of the *Dictionary of Americanisms* ([1877, p. 320](https://archive.org/details/dictionaryofamer00bart/page/320/mode/2up), CERT-2500-708) and John S. Farmer and W. E. Henley's *Slang and Its Analogues* (Vol. IV, [1896, p. 36](https://archive.org/details/slangitsanalogue04farmuoft/page/36/mode/2up), CERT-2500-806, recording variant *jimboree*). Subsequent historical-slang scholarship—led by Jonathan Lighter's *Historical Dictionary of American Slang* and Jonathon Green's [*Green's Dictionary of Slang*](https://greensdictofslang.com/entry/26363jq) (CERT-2500-904)—pushed the earliest common-noun baseline back to a June 1858 *Brooklyn Daily Times* courtroom item (`'a noisy drinking bout; a carousal, spree'`), dated the euchre card-table terms `jambone` (playing a lone hand with exposed cards) and `jamboree` (holding the five highest trumps) to the 1864 *American Hoyle*, dated benign `'merrymaking, celebration'` to 1870, dated Scouting usage to July 1919, dated the figurative `'medley, potpourri'` sense to 1985, and dated verbal `jamboree, v.` in *Green's* to June 4, 1905. Across all standard English dictionaries, the etymology remains flatly labeled **"of unknown origin."**

Two major specialist contributions over the past decade reshaped the research landscape and receive full priority credit in this dossier:

1. **Peter Reitan's Antebellum Minstrel Discovery (2018–2019)**: On the American Dialect Society mailing list ([ADS-L 154363, March 14, 2019](https://listserv.linguistlist.org/pipermail/ads-l/2019-March/154363.html), CERT-2500-905, summarizing his 2018 research), independent historical lexicographer Peter Reitan demonstrated that blackface minstrel composer Daniel Decatur Emmett performed a jig titled `"Whoop, Jam-bo-ree"` well before the 1858 Brooklyn murder trial, pointing to a July 3, 1850 *Pittsburgh Daily Post* theatre notice and T. B. Peterson's 1854 *White's New Ethiopian Song Book*. (Reitan also flagged a deceptive 1936 WPA *Annals of Cleveland* abstract that anachronistically paraphrased an 1860 news story using modern wording.)
2. **Anthony F. Buccini's Provençal / Louisiana French Hypothesis (2017–2018)**: In papers presented to the Dublin Gastronomy Symposium and the Oxford Symposium on Food and Cookery (*Seeds: Proceedings of the Oxford Symposium on Food and Cookery 2018*, CERT-2500-612), historical linguist Anthony F. Buccini argued that just as Louisiana French `jambalaya` ([May 1849 *American Agriculturist*, p. 161](https://archive.org/details/sim_american-agriculturist_1849-05_8_5/page/161/mode/1up), CERT-2500-607) continues Provençal `jambalaia` ('rice-and-fowl pilaf; messy mixture'), American `jamboree` reflects its Provençal/Occitan doublet `jambaraia` ('noisy mishmash, disorderly crowd'), transmitted up the Mississippi-Ohio riverboat corridor alongside `charivari` -> `shivaree` ([1848 Bartlett, p. 308](https://archive.org/details/dictionaryofamer00bartrich/page/308/mode/2up), CERT-2500-610).

Building on Reitan's and Buccini's work, Case 2500 executes a 10-domain primary-scan investigation across minstrel songsters and sheet music, maritime shanty collections, Northeast and Gulf/Western newspapers, *Hoyle* gaming manuals, Romance and Celtic/Scots donor lexicons, and pre-1858 archival false friends. All 93 evaluated records in the companion JSON ledger (`87` accompanied by pre-1931 public-domain page and masthead scans) separate empirical chronology from falsifiable linguistic derivation.

## 2. Antebellum Minstrelsy (`1849–1858`) and the June 1858 Brooklyn Murder Trial

### 2.1 Resolving the `"1854 Bryant's Minstrels"` Rumor and Dating *Christy's Panorama Songster* (`1855`)

Secondary musicological notes occasionally cite a purported `"c. 1854 Bryant's Minstrels"` sheet-music edition of Dan Emmett's `"Whoop, Jam-Bo-Ree."` Primary-scan inspection refutes the Bryant attribution on chronological grounds: Bryant's Minstrels did not form at Mechanics' Hall (472 Broadway) until February 1857, and Dan Emmett did not join the troupe until autumn 1858. Instead, the song originated during Emmett's 1849–1850 engagement at **Charles White's Melodeon** (53 Bowery, New York).

Two foundational songster and banjo-method scans anchor the antebellum stage text prior to the *OED*'s June 1858 baseline:

* **W. H. Murphy's *Christy's Panorama Songster* (`1855`, pre-May 1, 1856, CERT-2500-101)**: Printed by William H. Murphy at **384 Pearl Street, New York** ([pp. 135–136](https://archive.org/details/christyspanorama00unse/page/135/mode/2up)), this undated pocket songster carries the complete lyrics of `"WHOOP, JAM-BO-REE,—Jig. As sung by the original DAN EMMETT, (in imitation of the western boatmen,) at White's Melodeon."` Cross-checking *Doggett's* and *Trow's New York City Directories* fixes Murphy's occupancy at `384 Pearl Street` strictly between May 1849 and **May 1, 1856**, when his printing house permanently relocated to `372 Pearl Street`. Because neighboring pages include songs introduced in 1854–1855, this impression dates securely to **`1855` (and bibliographically no later than April 30, 1856)**—antedating the *OED*'s 1858 baseline by **3 years** on an open public-domain scan. Every stanza punctuates a raucous street and riverboat spree with the trisyllabic oxytonic refrain `"Whoop, jam-bo-ree!"`
* **Trap A Imprint Audit of *White's New Ethiopian Song Book* (`post-1857` H. Long & Brother Reissue, CERT-2500-102)**: Although T. B. Peterson registered the first copyright of *White's New Ethiopian Song Book* in 1854, visual inspection of the widely digitized H. Long & Brother copy ([pp. 81–82](https://archive.org/details/whitesnewethiopi00whit/page/80/mode/2up)) reveals its `121 Nassau-street` imprint, which Long occupied only from May 1857 onward—demonstrating why physical imprint verification is indispensable even on genuine antebellum texts. Earlier minstrel and banjo manuals—including *White's New Illustrated Melodeon Song Book* ([1848](https://archive.org/details/whitesnewillustr00whit/page/n5/mode/2up), CERT-2500-103) and Thomas F. Briggs's *Banjo Instructor* ([1855](https://archive.org/details/briggsbanjoinstr00brig/page/n3/mode/2up), CERT-2500-104)—contain **zero** attestations of `jamboree`, pinpointing Emmett's 1849–1850 Melodeon act as the exact theatrical vector.
* **Phil. Rice's *Correct Method for the Banjo* (`1858`, CERT-2500-105)**: Entered for Massachusetts District Court copyright by Oliver Ditson & Co. in 1858 ([p. 55](https://archive.org/details/phil-rices-method-for-the-banjo-with-or-without-a-master-1858/page/n53/mode/2up)), Rice's tutor prints the complete notated 2/4 G-major instrumental score of `"Whoop Jamboree"`, confirming that `"Jam-bo-ree"` functioned simultaneously as a syncopated western-boatman refrain and named jig tune by 1858, and remained a stock minstrel air (`AIR—"Jamboree."`) in *Frank Brower's Black Diamond Songster* ([March 3, 1864, p. 35](https://archive.org/details/frankbrowersblac00brow/page/35/mode/2up), CERT-2500-106).

### 2.2 Reconstructing the June 15, 1858 Brooklyn Murder Trial (*The People v. Patrick Lally*)

The *OED*'s unrevised 1858 baseline cites a brief quotation from the *Brooklyn Daily Times* without identifying the legal proceeding. A multi-paper sweep reconstructs the exact courtroom event—the Kings County Court of Oyer and Terminer trial of **Patrick Lally** on **June 15, 1858** for the fatal beating of his wife Mary Lally in Brooklyn—through two independent stenographic transcripts published the next morning:

* ***The New-York Times* (June 16, 1858, [p. 5, col. 3](https://archive.org/details/sim_new-york-times_the-new-york-times_1858-06-16_7_2103/page/n4/mode/1up), CERT-2500-302)**: Prosecution witness **John H.McCormick** testifies that Lally came into his saloon asking about noise the night before (`"had a kind of jam-beree the night before"`), and under judicial questioning defines the unfamiliar slang word explicitly: `"'Jamberee' means a fight or drunk."`
* ***The New York Herald* (June 17, 1858, [p. 2, col. 5](https://www.loc.gov/resource/sn83030313/1858-06-17/ed-1/?sp=2), CERT-2500-303)**: Prints the standard `-oree` spelling (`"jamboree"`) twice, records `[Laughter]` in the courtroom when the witness explains `"a jamboree means a drunk or a fight"`, and preserves defense counsel's cross-examination asking whether the witness picked up the word among **volunteer firemen**—establishing New York/Brooklyn saloon and fire-company culture as the exact urban habitat where Emmett's stage refrain lexicalized into a count noun (`"a jamboree"`).

By late 1861 and January 1862—still prior to any card-game manual—New York comic weekly *Vanity Fair* deployed nominal `"jamboree"` unglossed for a noisy drunken spree ([Dec. 28, 1861, p. 291](https://archive.org/details/sim_vanity-fair_1861-12-28_4_105/page/291/mode/1up), CERT-2500-304; [Jan. 25, 1862, p. 47](https://archive.org/details/sim_vanity-fair_1862-01-25_5_109/page/47/mode/1up), CERT-2500-305).

## 3. Resolving Register Priority: Saloon Brawl vs. Euchre Card Table (`1862`) and Sea Shanty (`1913–1920`)

### 3.1 Edition-by-Edition *Hoyle* Audit: Euchre `Jambone` and `Jamboree` Antedated to March 1862

A longstanding puzzle in American lexicography is the relationship between saloon `jamboree` (`1858`) and the twin euchre declarations `jambone` (playing a lone hand with cards exposed on the table for 8 points, *OED* `1864`) and `jamboree` (combining the five highest trump cards in one hand for 16 points, *OED* `1864`). Did the card table coin `jamboree` as a playful phonetic deformation of `jambon` / `jambone`, or did card players borrow an existing saloon noun?

An edition-by-edition primary-scan audit settles the chronology decisively in favor of **saloon-brawl priority (`1850–1858` > `1862`)** while antedating both *OED* euchre entries by **2 years**:

### 3.2 Refuting Maritime Shanty Priority (`1879–1928` Canon Sweep vs. `1913–1920` Print Debut)

Popular folk-song commentary frequently asserts that `"Whoop Jamboree"` (`"Whip Jamboree"`) was an 1840s Irish packet-ship capstan shanty that gave American English the noun `jamboree`. Primary-scan testing overturns maritime priority completely:

1. **Zero Hits Across 11 Canonical Shanty Collections (`1879–1928`, CERT-2500-204)**: Exhaustive full-text and index inspection across Laura Alexandrine Smith's [*The Music of the Waters* (1888)](https://archive.org/details/musicwaters00smit/page/34/mode/2up), R. C. Adams (1879), W. L. Alden (1882), S. B. Luce (1883), F. J. Davis & F. Tozer (1887), John Masefield (1906), Capt. W. B. Whall's 1st–3rd editions (1910–1913), Bullen & Arnold (1914), Joanna Colcord (1924), C. Fox Smith (1927), and F. P. Harlow (1928) yields **zero** occurrences of `jamboree`, `whoop jamboree`, or `whip jamboree`.
2. **Shore-Port Slang Before Shanty Text (`1875–1897`)**: Pre-1900 maritime writers use `jamboree` strictly as shore-leave drinking slang—including the [*Galveston Daily News* (July 29, 1875, p. 4)](https://www.loc.gov/resource/sn84020214/1875-07-29/ed-1/?sp=4) (CERT-2500-202), Samuel C. Upham's retrospective *Notes of a Voyage to California via Cape Horn* ([1878, p. 221](https://archive.org/details/notesofvoyagetoc00upha/page/220/mode/2up), CERT-2500-203, composed in 1878 rather than an original 1849 shipboard log), and Herbert Elliott Hamblen's *On Many Seas* ([1897, p. 114](https://archive.org/details/onmanyseaslifeex00hamb/page/114/mode/2up), CERT-2500-206)—while an alleged April 25, 1868 *Army and Navy Journal* hit dissolves on scan into a dirty-OCR ghost (CERT-2500-201).
3. **First Shanty Print (`Dec. 12, 1913`) & Correction of `"Whall 1910"` to `Feb. 1920`**: The earliest dated print text of the sea shanty appears in `"A Capstan Chanty: From Memories of Sixty Years Ago"` in the [*New-York Tribune* (Dec. 12, 1913, p. 8)](https://www.loc.gov/resource/sn83030214/1913-12-12/ed-1/?sp=8) (`"Jamboree, oh, jamboree"`, CERT-2500-207), followed by Cecil J. Sharp's *English Folk-Chanteys* ([1914, pp. 42–43, 71](https://archive.org/details/englishfolkchant00shar/page/42/mode/2up), `"Whip Jamboree"`, taken down from Somerset singer John Short in August 1912, CERT-2500-208) and Sir Richard Runciman Terry's *The Shanty Book, Part II* ([1926, pp. 38–39](https://archive.org/details/shantybookpartii00terr/page/38/mode/2up), `"Whoop Jamboree"`, CERT-2500-212). Crucially, citations of Capt. W. B. Whall's `"1910"` shanty book for `"Jamboree"` reflect a bibliographic misdate: `JAMBOREE` was added only in Whall's posthumous **Fourth Edition** ([Feb. 1920, pp. xii, 127](https://archive.org/details/seasongsshanties00whal/page/126/mode/2up), CERT-2500-211), where the headnote explicitly states that sailors adapted the chorus from an **American minstrel song**.

## 4. Semantic Bleaching, Word-Family Radiation, and the Boy Scout Jamboree (`1864–1920`)

During and immediately after the Civil War, `jamboree` broadened rapidly from urban saloon brawls to organized public festivities, musical medleys, finite verbs, tall-tale compounds, and international youth rallies:

* **Benign Public `'Merrymaking / Entertainment'` (`June 25, 1864`, 5 yrs, 6 mos earlier than *OED* `1870`)**: In [*The New York Clipper* (June 25, 1864, p. 83)](https://archive.org/details/per_new-york-clipper_new-york-clipper_1864-06-25_12_11/page/n2/mode/1up) (CERT-2500-307), theatrical and sporting reporters applied `jamboree` to a ticketed public pugilistic/variety benefit at Manhattan's Stuyvesant Institute. Seven weeks later, the *Clipper* paired `"DOONEY HARRIS JUBILEE"` in the headline with `"jambaree"` in the body ([Aug. 6, 1864, p. 134](https://archive.org/details/per_new-york-clipper_new-york-clipper_1864-08-06_12_17/page/n5/mode/1up), CERT-2500-107—supplying direct primary-scan proof of prosodic cross-association with *jubilee*), followed one week later by an unviolent civic celebration in Elmira, New York: `"Grand Emancipation 'Jamboree.'"` ([Aug. 13, 1864, p. 143](https://archive.org/details/per_new-york-clipper_new-york-clipper_1864-08-13_12_18/page/n6/mode/1up), CERT-2500-108) and a May 6, 1865 minstrel/stage item ([p. 28](https://archive.org/details/per_new-york-clipper_new-york-clipper_1865-05-06_13_3/page/n3/mode/1up), CERT-2500-109). Mainstream dailies followed in the [*New-York Times* (Sept. 1, 1865, p. 4)](https://archive.org/details/sim_new-york-times_the-new-york-times_1865-09-01_14_4347/page/n3/mode/1up) (CERT-2500-310) and Philadelphia [*Daily Evening Telegraph* (July 7, 1866, p. 5)](https://www.loc.gov/resource/sn83025925/1866-07-07/ed-1/?sp=5) (CERT-2500-311).
* **Inland River, Southern, and Pacific-Slope Diffusion (`1864–1875`)**: While our sweep of pre-1861 Gulf and Gold Rush papers confirms zero antebellum Southern/Western common-noun hits, railroad and riverboat exchanges carried `jamboree` to the [*Chicago Daily Tribune* (April 28, 1864, p. 4)](https://www.loc.gov/resource/sn84031490/1864-04-28/ed-1/?sp=4) (CERT-2500-401), East Texas in [*The Harrison Flag* (Jan. 11, 1868, p. 2)](https://www.loc.gov/resource/sn86088544/1868-01-11/ed-1/?sp=2) (spelled `"Jam-Bo-Ree"`, preserving Emmett's three-part minstrel hyphenation, CERT-2500-402), the [*Daily Memphis Avalanche* (March 26, 1868, p. 3)](https://www.loc.gov/resource/sn83045160/1868-03-26/ed-1/?sp=3) (CERT-2500-403), [*The Atlanta Constitution* (March 3, 1872, p. 3)](https://archive.org/details/per_atlanta-constitution_the-atlanta-constitution_1872-03-03_4_222/page/n2/mode/1up) (CERT-2500-405), and Nevada mining towns in the [*Daily Nevada State Journal* (March 28, 1875, p. 3)](https://www.loc.gov/resource/sn84022046/1875-03-28/ed-1/?sp=3) (CERT-2500-408). By New Year's Eve 1875, nationwide press headlines hailed 1876 as `"The Centennial Jamboree"` in the [*Cincinnati Daily Star* (Dec. 27, 1875, p. 1)](https://www.loc.gov/resource/sn85025759/1875-12-27/ed-1/?sp=1) (CERT-2500-803) and Unionville, Nevada [*Silver State* (Dec. 31, 1875, p. 2)](https://www.loc.gov/resource/sn84022060/1875-12-31/ed-1/?sp=2) (CERT-2500-804), cementing its respectability as a mass civic festival.
* **Verbal `jamboree, v.` (`Feb. 12, 1875`, 30 yrs, 3 mos earlier than *Green's* `1905`)**: Unrecorded in 19th-century *OED* coverage and dated in *Green's Dictionary of Slang* to June 4, 1905, finite verbal conversion appears in [*The New Orleans Bulletin* (Feb. 12, 1875, p. 6, col. 3)](https://www.loc.gov/resource/sn86079018/1875-02-12/ed-1/?sp=6) (`"had jamboreed around"`, CERT-2500-801), reprinted sixteen days later in the [*New York Dispatch* (Feb. 28, 1875, p. 7)](https://www.loc.gov/resource/sn85026214/1875-02-28/ed-1/?sp=7) (CERT-2500-802), and followed by participial `"went jamboreeing"` in the [*Cincinnati Daily Star* (Jan. 4, 1876, p. 4)](https://www.loc.gov/resource/sn85025759/1876-01-04/ed-1/?sp=4) (CERT-2500-805).
* **Musical `'Medley / Potpourri'` (`1878`, 107 yrs earlier than *OED* `1985`), Twain's `whoopjamboreehoo` (`1884–1885`), and Australian `jamberoo` (`1889`)**: Charles A. White's Boston piano score [*Jamboree: Medley for the Piano-Forte* (1878)](https://www.loc.gov/item/2023830225/) (CERT-2500-111) and E. Beyer's [*Musical "Pow-Wow." Serio-Comic Jamboree* (Aug. 5, 1881)](https://www.loc.gov/item/sm1881.11604/) (CERT-2500-112) push the `'heterogeneous medley'` sense back more than a century prior to *OED*'s 1985 citation. In Chapter XXXIX of *Huckleberry Finn*, first-edition page scans prove that Mark Twain printed Tom Sawyer's expressive compound as a single unhyphenated italic word—**`whoopjamboreehoo`**—in both the London Chatto & Windus first edition ([Dec. 10, 1884, p. 385](https://archive.org/details/ost-english-the_adventures_of_huckleberry_finn/page/n423/mode/1up), CERT-2500-410) and the New York Charles L. Webster first American edition ([Feb. 18, 1885, p. 338](https://archive.org/details/adventureshuckle00twaiiala/page/338/mode/1up), CERT-2500-411), fusing Emmett's `"Whoop, jam-bo-ree"` refrain with frontier `-hoo` (`ballyhoo`). Across the Pacific, Sidney J. Baker's *The Australian Language* (1945, p. 176, CERT-2500-809) documents Australian slang remodeling `jamboree` into `jamberoo` ('noisy spree', 1889; *Sydney Bulletin*, Jan. 8, 1898) by local attraction to `-eroo` and NSW *Jamberoo*.
* **Baden-Powell's `international jamboree` (`May 14, 1919`) and `"North American Indians"` Myth-Seeding (`Jan. 1920`)**: Whereas *OED* dates Scouting `jamboree` to July 1919 in the British *Headquarters Gazette*, Lieutenant-General Sir Robert Baden-Powell publicly announced the name two months earlier upon stepping ashore in the United States, telling Washington reporters in [*The Evening Star* (May 14, 1919, p. 30, col. 1)](https://www.loc.gov/resource/sn83045462/1919-05-14/ed-1/?sp=30) that British and American Scouts would hold `"an international jamboree in London"` in 1920 (CERT-2500-807). Eight months later, Chief Scout Executive James E. West's announcement in [*Boys' Life* (Jan. 1920, p. 41)](https://archive.org/details/sim_boys-life_1920-01_10_1/page/41/mode/1up) quoted Baden-Powell proposing `"a 'Jamboree' after the example of the North American Indians"` (CERT-2500-808)—catching in primary print the exact moment Baden-Powell inadvertently seeded the 20th-century Native American folk etymology, before later claiming in *The Outlook* ([Oct. 1920](https://archive.org/details/sim_new-outlook_1920-10-06_126_6/page/238/mode/2up), CERT-2500-912) that people elsewhere did not even know the British/American word.

## 5. Evaluating Origin Pathways, Folk-Myth Refutations, and Trap A–D Exclusions

### 5.1 Convergent Donor Contact and Expressive `-oree` / `-aree` Morphology

Primary-scan testing of Romance, British/Scots, and Irish dictionaries clarifies both the strengths and archival limits of competing linguistic proposals:

1. **Provençal `jambaraia` / Louisiana French `jambalaia` + `shivaree` (CERT-2500-601, CERT-2500-603 to CERT-2500-610)**: Scanning 18th- and early-19th-century Provençal dictionaries reveals an important chronological nuance: `jambaraia` is absent from Pierre-Augustin Boissier de Sauvages's *Dictionnaire languedocien-françois* ([1785, Vol. II, p. 11](https://archive.org/details/dictionnairelang02boisuoft/page/10/mode/2up), which records only distant `JHIMBÊLÔTO`, CERT-2500-601), J.-T. Avril's *Dictionnaire provençal-français* ([1839, p. 76](https://archive.org/details/dictionnaireprov00avri/page/76/mode/2up), `CHEREVELIN`, CERT-2500-603), and Simon-Jude Honnorat's *Dictionnaire provençal-français* ([1847, Vol. II, p. 475](https://archive.org/details/dictionnaireprov02honn/page/474/mode/2up), `JAMBINETA`, CERT-2500-604). However, Marseille poet Fortuné Chailan's vernacular poem `"Leis Amours de Vanus"` (composed 1837; collected in [*Lou Gangui*, 4th ed., 1894, p. 51](https://archive.org/details/lougangui00chai/page/50/mode/2up), CERT-2500-605) uses Provençal **`jambaraya`** directly amid tipsy revelers, and Frédéric Mistral's *Lou Tresor dóu Felibrige* ([Vol. II, Aug. 1886, p. 151](https://archive.org/details/loutresordoufel02mist/page/150/mode/2up), CERT-2500-606) lemmatizes **`JAMBARAIA`** (`'méli-mélo, cohue, foule désordonnée'`) right beside culinary `JAMBALAIA`. In North America, Solon Robinson's `"Hopping John"` recipe in the *American Agriculturist* (May 1849, CERT-2500-607) proves Provençal `jambalaia` had already entered Gulf Coast English by 1849—later rendered **`Jumberlie`** (`[dʒʌmbə(r)ˈliː]`) by formerly enslaved Mobile/San Francisco cook Abby Fisher in *What Mrs. Fisher Knows About Old Southern Cooking* ([1881, p. 52](https://archive.org/details/whatmrsfisherkno00fish/page/52/mode/2up), CERT-2500-608), showing exact unstressed medial vowel reduction and `-ee` oxytonic anglicization—alongside Mississippi Valley `sherri-varries` (John Fanning Watson's 1804–1805 New Orleans diary, printed [May 1843, p. 228](https://archive.org/details/sim_american-pioneer_1843-05_2_5/page/228/mode/2up), CERT-2500-609) and `shevaree` (Bartlett 1848, CERT-2500-610).
2. **English `jam` + Productive American `-oree` / `-aree` Remodeling (CERT-2500-701 to CERT-2500-707)**: British and Celtic single-source etyma fall short phonologically: Lowland Scots `bourach` ('confused crowd', Jamieson [1808](https://archive.org/details/etymologicaldict01jami/page/n293/mode/2up), CERT-2500-701) lacks the `jam-` onset, and Irish `geamaire` ('masked wedding mummer in Roscommon; prattler', Dinneen [1904, pp. 355–356](https://archive.org/details/foclirgaeilgeagu00dinn/page/355/mode/2up), CERT-2500-703) has palatalized `/ɟ/`, initial stress, and no bilabial `/b/`. Conversely, English regional and colloquial `jam` ('crush, tight crowd, noisy press', Wright *EDD* Vol. III [1902, p. 345](https://archive.org/details/englishdialectdi03wriguoft/page/344/mode/2up), CERT-2500-702) and American oxytonic `-oree` / `-aree` formations—attested antebellum in `"cutting up all sorts of jigamarees"` in the [*New-Haven Daily Herald* (Feb. 5, 1844, p. 2)](https://www.loc.gov/resource/sn82015816/1844-02-05/ed-1/?sp=2) (CERT-2500-704), `shivaree`, `bobbery` (*Hobson-Jobson* [1903, p. 101](https://archive.org/details/hobsonjobsonaglo00yulerich/page/100/mode/2up), CERT-2500-909), and minstrel `jubilee`—supplied the exact prosodic mold (`ˌdʒæmbəˈriː`) in which Dan Emmett framed `"Whoop, Jam-bo-ree! (in imitation of the western boatmen)"`. Crucially, negative-corpus sweeps of George W. Matsell's *Vocabulum; or, The Rogue's Lexicon* ([May 23, 1859 1st ed., p. 47](https://archive.org/details/vocabulumorrogue00mats/page/46/mode/2up), CERT-2500-705), Bartlett's 1859 2nd ed. ([p. 218](https://archive.org/details/dictionaryofamer00bart_0/page/218/mode/2up), CERT-2500-706), and John Camden Hotten's *Slang Dictionary* ([1874 ed., p. 201](https://archive.org/details/slangdictionarye00hott/page/200/mode/2up), CERT-2500-707) confirm that `jamboree` did **not** originate in London/New York thieves' cant (`0` hits), corroborating its emergence out of western-boatman and Bowery minstrel performance.

### 5.2 Falsified Folk Myths and Pre-1858 Trap A–D Archival Audit

Finally, primary-scan inspection refutes five recurring popular etymologies and eliminates a dozen deceptive pre-1858 database ghosts:

* **Five Falsified Folk-Etymology Myths**:
  1. *Swahili `jambo` ('hello', Krapf [1882, p. 112](https://archive.org/details/dictionaryofsuah00krap/page/112/mode/2up), CERT-2500-907)*: East African greeting absent from antebellum U.S. contact; popularized only after 1930s East African Scout rallies.
  2. *Hindi/Urdu `jumhūr` ('concourse, republic', Shakespear [1834, p. 618](https://archive.org/details/dictionaryhindus00shak/page/n319/mode/2up), CERT-2500-908)*: Unattested as `jamboree` in Anglo-Indian glossaries including Yule & Burnell's *Hobson-Jobson* (1903, CERT-2500-909), with zero colonial India bridge prior to 1850–1858 New York usage.
  3. *Native American / Choctaw "tribal gathering" myth (Byington [1915](https://archive.org/details/dictionaryofchoc00byin/page/n7/mode/2up), CERT-2500-910)*: Zero phonetic or semantic root in Choctaw or Algonquian lexicons; traced directly to Baden-Powell's Jan. 1920 *Boys' Life* remark (CERT-2500-808).
  4. *Jamberoo, New South Wales toponym (Wells [1848, p. 214](https://archive.org/details/geographicaldict00wellrich/page/214/mode/2up), CERT-2500-911)*: Dharawal Aboriginal parish name (`jambaroo`, 'footpath/track') near Kiama, NSW; Australian slang `jamberoo` ('spree') appears only in 1889–1898, four decades after Emmett's 1850–1855 songster.
  5. *Baden-Powell 1919–1920 personal coinage myth (CERT-2500-912)*: Refuted by seventy years of continuous American and British print (`1850–1919`).
* **Pre-1858 Trap A–D False Friends Eliminated**:
  * **Trap A (Serial Founding-Year & Reel-Splice Misdates)**: `"1857"` *Atlantic Monthly* -> Dec. 1903 Vol. XCII ([p. 816](https://archive.org/details/sim_atlantic-monthly_1903-12_92_554/page/816/mode/1up), CERT-2500-951); `"1841"` *Punch* -> May 18, 1895 Vol. CVIII ([p. 237](https://archive.org/details/punchvol108a109lemouoft/page/236/mode/2up), CERT-2500-952); `"1856"` *Lowell Daily Courier* -> Oct. 26, 1881 microfilm splice (CERT-2500-953); `"1864"` *The Telegrapher* -> Dec. 11, 1875 Vol. XI ([p. 298](https://archive.org/details/telegrapher1112nati/page/298/mode/2up), CERT-2500-412); `"1850"` *Mercantile Navy List* -> May 1894 yacht *Jamboree* ([p. 217](https://archive.org/details/mercantilenavyli1894grea/page/216/mode/2up), CERT-2500-954).
  * **Trap B (Dirty-OCR Ghosts)**: July 30, 1849 *Pittsburgh Daily Gazette* `"Jamborees"` -> physical ink **`discharges`** ([p. 2](https://www.loc.gov/resource/sn83045366/1849-07-30/ed-1/?sp=2), CERT-2500-312); Sept. 12, 1853 *Daily Pittsburgh Gazette* `"Jamboree"` -> broken commercial type ([p. 2](https://www.loc.gov/resource/sn83045366/1853-09-12/ed-1/?sp=2), CERT-2500-955); 1809 Nicholson *British Encyclopedia* `"jamboree"` -> **`tambourine`** ([Vol. V](https://archive.org/details/britishencyclope05nich/page/n315/mode/2up), CERT-2500-957); 1821 Weimar travelogue `"Gamboree"` -> Fraktur **`Tamboree`** / drum ([p. 112](https://archive.org/details/neuestelnderundv21ehrm/page/112/mode/2up), CERT-2500-958).
  * **Trap C & D (Toponym Homographs & Post-1930 Intrusions)**: 1815 Mungo Park *Journal of a Mission to the Interior of Africa* `"Jamberoo"` -> West African Bambara village name ([p. 139](https://archive.org/details/journalofmission00park/page/138/mode/2up), CERT-2500-961); `"1850s"` Foreign Office archive hit -> 1958 modern Cold War African summit header (`FO 407`, CERT-2500-962).
