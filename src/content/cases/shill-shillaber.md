## 1. Prior Scholarship and the 1883 Chicago Mock-Auction Origin

Standard dictionaries long treated *shillaber* and its clipped derivative *shill* as twentieth-century carnival slang of unknown origin. In the [*Oxford English Dictionary*](https://www.oed.com/dictionary/shillaber_n) (*OED3*, retaining unrevised 1986 *Supplement* entries), *shillaber, n.* opens on 6 December 1913 in [*Collier's* (p. 29, col. 2)](https://archive.org/details/colliers52sepdec1913newy/page/n455/mode/1up) (`CERT-1200-113`), [*shill, v.³*](https://www.oed.com/dictionary/shill_v3) opens in 1914 in Louis E. Jackson and C. R. Hellyer's [*A Vocabulary of Criminal Slang* (p. 75)](https://archive.org/details/vocabularyofcrim00jackrich/page/75/mode/1up) (`CERT-1200-114`), and [*shill, n.*](https://www.oed.com/dictionary/shill_n) opens in 1916 (*The Editor*, echoed in [*Merriam-Webster*](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/shill)). Jonathan E. Lighter's [*Random House Historical Dictionary of American Slang*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Historical_Dictionary_of_American_Slang) (*HDAS*, Vols. 1–2, *A–O*, 1994–1997) documented the nineteenth-century confederate predecessors *booster* and *capper*, but its projected *P–Z* volumes remained unpublished, leaving *shill* and *shillaber* untreated (as did Peter Tamony's twentieth-century slips in [SHSMO C3939, Folder 520](https://files.shsmo.org/manuscripts/columbia/C3939.pdf)).

Two scholarly advances substantially narrowed the gap. Researcher Sven Yargs ([English StackExchange Q62859](https://english.stackexchange.com/questions/62859/origin-of-the-word-shill-shillaber), 2016), credited in Douglas Harper's [*Online Etymology Dictionary*](https://www.etymonline.com/word/shill), pushed carnival *shillabers* to a 15 February 1908 *Los Angeles Herald* item on cane-and-ring rack assistants (`CERT-1200-120`) and nominal *shills* to Robert Brown's April 1911 *Metropolitan Magazine* story "The Watch." Most decisively, Jonathon Green's [*Green's Dictionary of Slang*](https://greensdictofslang.com/entry/jm5glaq) (*s.v. shill, n.²*) uncovered an isolated nineteenth-century quotation in a western Pennsylvania weekly—the *Indiana Weekly Messenger* of 14 March 1883 (p. 1, col. 5)—before a thirty-one-year jump to 1914.

Because Green's 1883 Pennsylvania snippet lacked a dateline or credit, where *shillaber* was actually being spoken remained unknown. Phrase-matching Green's text across Midwestern newspaper exchanges recovers the complete two-column exposé **49 days earlier** in [*The Janesville Daily Gazette* (24 January 1883, p. 3, cols. 2–3)](https://archive.org/details/janesville-daily-gazette-1883-01-24/page/n2/mode/1up) (`CERT-1200-101` — 30 years and 10 months before *OED* 1913). Headlined `"MOCK AUCTIONS."` and signed **`—Chicago Times.`**, it prints *shillaber* and *shillabers* **fourteen times** (plus one typo, *shallabers*) alongside urban watch-swindling cant (*s. g. watch*, *yellow super*, *slang*, *capping*, and *guys*):

> MOCK AUCTIONS. There is a red flag at the door. The room is always small, with an office at one end where the swindled purchaser pays for what he buys, and so situated as to protect the shillaber in his sham purchase or the victim from the gaze of the crowd, for it is not prudent to have two "guys" at a time at the cashier's desk. The auctioneer, whose buzz-saw voice attracts the passers-by, is selling a watch. It is what the "shillabers" call an "s. g. watch," meaning a silver-gilt watch.

Five months later, an independent local police report in [*The Chicago Daily Tribune* (7 June 1883, p. 8, col. 3)](https://archive.org/details/per_chicago-daily-tribune_the-chicago-daily-tribun_1883-06-07_42/page/n7/mode/1up) (`CERT-1200-102`) corroborated active municipal usage at two Loop addresses preying on visitors to the National Exposition of Railway Appliances:

> The proprietors of the shops at No. 110 Clark street and No. 300 State street have both been under arrest during the last month on complaint of strangers who have been roped-in and robbed by the clarion-voiced auctioneer and his gang of "shillabers."

Searching all 513 digitized circus route books (1872–1912) at Illinois State University's Milner Library (`CERT-1200-118`, `CERT-1200-206`) yielded zero tokens of *shillaber* or *shill* (annex rosters list *Outside Ticket Box* and *ballyhoo*). Because printed route books are formal rosters rather than spoken midway transcripts, that sweep provides supporting register context rather than negative proof; the positive 1883 Chicago mock-auction records establish the urban starting point directly.

## 2. Storefront Prosopography: No. 300 State Street, No. 110 Clark Street, and Threshold-Crossing Cappers

Municipal dockets and directories between 1880 and 1885 reconstruct the exact bilingual network operating `No. 300 State street` and `No. 110 Clark street`:

* **No. 300 South State Street (`CERT-1400-109`, `CERT-1400-110`, `CERT-1400-113`):** Formerly pawnbroker Louis Schloessinger's shop ([*Chicago Daily Tribune*, 13 November 1880, p. 3](https://archive.org/details/per_chicago-daily-tribune_the-chicago-daily-tribun_1880-11-13_40/page/n2/mode/1up)), 300 South State Street operated across 1882–1884 as a watch mock-auction house run by German-Jewish proprietors **Max ("M.") Levi (Levy)** and **Nathan Levi** with ex-pawnbroker **Sam Abrahams**, **Henry Berg & Son** ([23 September 1882, p. 9](https://archive.org/details/per_chicago-daily-tribune_the-chicago-daily-tribun_1882-09-23_42/page/n8/mode/1up)), and Yankee auctioneer **Ed Leavitt** (later Isaac Short in [*Marquis' Hand-Book of Chicago*, 1886–87, leaf n302](https://archive.org/details/anmarquiscoshand00marq/page/n301/mode/2up)). On [30 August 1882 (p. 8, col. 3)](https://archive.org/details/per_chicago-daily-tribune_the-chicago-daily-tribun_1882-08-30_42/page/n7/mode/1up) (`CERT-1400-109`), the *Tribune* warned that *"No. 300 State street, where a mock auction is run by M. Levy and a gang of cappers, duly licensed by the Mayor, is a good place for 'suckers' to steer clear of"*, after Ed Leavitt sold visitor Peter Lawler a brass watch for $30; on [31 January 1884 (p. 3, col. 5)](https://archive.org/details/per_chicago-daily-tribune_1884-01-31_44/page/n2/mode/1up) (`CERT-1400-110`), police traced stolen goods to *"Sam Abrahams and Nathan & Max Levi, who had an auction-store at 300 State street"* and a branch in Kansas City.
* **No. 110 South Clark Street and Sister Loop Shops (`CERT-1400-111`, `CERT-1400-112`, `CERT-1400-113`):** Ground-floor stalls of the Exchange Building at 108–110 South Clark Street (demolished May 1884, [6 February 1884, p. 12](https://archive.org/details/per_chicago-daily-tribune_1884-02-06_44/page/n11/mode/1up)) housed merchant tailor **P. J. Saulson & Co.** ([2 November 1882, p. 1](https://archive.org/details/per_chicago-daily-tribune_the-chicago-daily-tribun_1882-11-02_42/page/n0/mode/1up)), saloonkeeper Edward Gray ([26 April 1883, p. 7](https://archive.org/details/per_chicago-daily-tribune_the-chicago-daily-tribun_1883-04-26_42/page/n6/mode/1up)), and daily-permit watch auctions under the [City Council's 1882–83 permit ordinance (leaf n515)](https://archive.org/details/proceedingsofcit17chic/page/n515/mode/1up) (`CERT-1400-113`), one block from **H. C. Kleinsmith** and **Henry Meyers** on South Clark Street ([5 June 1885, p. 8](https://archive.org/details/per_chicago-daily-tribune_the-chicago-daily-tribun_1885-06-05_45/page/n7/mode/1up)) and **Bernard and Arnold Marks** on Madison Street ([21 July 1882, p. 8](https://archive.org/details/per_chicago-daily-tribune_the-chicago-daily-tribun_1882-07-21_41/page/n7/mode/1up), `CERT-1400-112`).

Unlike clothing-store `"pullers-in"` who stayed outside on the sidewalk ([*Chicago Daily Tribune*, 20 December 1885, p. 19](https://archive.org/details/per_chicago-daily-tribune_the-chicago-daily-tribun_1885-12-20_45/page/n18/mode/1up), `CERT-1400-117`), Loop mock-auction confederates crossed directly from sidewalk bystanders to the bidding counter. [*The Chicago Daily Tribune* (19 September 1883, p. 8, col. 2)](https://archive.org/details/per_chicago-daily-tribune_1883-09-19_42/page/n7/mode/1up) (`CERT-1400-111`) documents Clark Street operative **Ed Roth ("the Pretty Capper")** uniting outside steerer and inside decoy bidder:

> Ed Roth, known to the police as "the Pretty Capper," was last evening arrested by Detective Kay in front of Kleinsmith's jewelry auction store on Clark street, between Adams and Monroe. ... Good-looking and well-dressed, he enters the store in the presence of bystanders and presents a watch or some article of jewelry, upon which he expresses a desire to realize some money. ... The credulous onlookers, supposing the transaction to be bona fide, bid on the article, which is represented as one of value, but which proves to be worthless, it having been given the "capper" for just such a purpose by the proprietor of the place.

This bilingual storefront partnership also explains Jackson and Hellyer's ([1914, p. 75](https://archive.org/details/vocabularyofcrim00jackrich/page/75/mode/1up), `CERT-1200-114`) cross-reference `SHILLIBER. Current among modern thieves. See Shoniker` (Yiddish/Rotwelsch cant for a Jewish street-trader or novice).

## 3. Bridging the 1884–1907 Print Gap and Unmasking Both 1910–1911 Syndicates

Between July 1883 and January 1908, mainstream newspaper editors routinely substituted *cappers* and *steerers* (`CERT-1200-208`, `CERT-1200-212`, `CERT-1200-213`), including in coverage of gambling dens outside the 1893 Chicago World's Fair ([*Chicago Daily Tribune*, 23 September 1893, p. 1](https://archive.org/details/per_chicago-daily-tribune_the-chicago-daily-tribun_1893-09-23_52_266/page/n0/mode/1up), `CERT-1400-214`) and George Appo's 1894 Lexow testimony ([*The Argonaut*, 8 October 1894, p. 1](https://archive.org/details/argonaut351894sanf/page/n233/mode/1up), `CERT-1400-215`). When pitchmen carried mock-auction slang into outdoor show weeklies in early 1908, *shillaber* surfaced simultaneously in Los Angeles (`15 Feb 1908`, `CERT-1200-120`), Cincinnati ([*The Billboard*, 7 March 1908, p. 24](https://archive.org/details/sim_billboard_1908-03-07_20_10/page/n23/mode/1up), `CERT-1200-103`), and New York ([*The New York Clipper*, 14 March 1908, p. 119](https://archive.org/details/per_new-york-clipper_new-york-clipper_1908-03-14_56_4/page/n18/mode/1up), `CERT-1400-208`, 5 years and 9 months before *OED* 1913).

Rapid morphological clipping and register diffusion followed across 1909–1911 (`CERT-1200-104`, `CERT-1200-105`, `CERT-1200-205`, `CERT-1200-214`, `CERT-1200-216`). In [*The Saturday Evening Post* (14 May 1910, pp. 12, 14)](https://archive.org/details/sim_saturday-evening-post_1910-05-14_182_46/page/n13/mode/1up) (`CERT-1200-106`, `CERT-1200-119`, `CERT-1200-215`), George Randolph Chester introduced verbal *shill through* and preterite *shilled* (*"The mayor shilled in the first openin'"*, 3 years and 7 months before *OED* 1914; compare bare *shill* in [*Everybody's Magazine*, May 1911, p. 605](https://archive.org/details/sim_everybodys-magazine_1911-05_24_5/page/n26/mode/1up), `CERT-1200-110`), followed on [9 July 1910 (p. 5)](https://archive.org/details/sim_saturday-evening-post_1910-07-09_183_2/page/n6/mode/1up) by agentive *Shillers* (`CERT-1200-107`, 18 years and 9 months before *Green's* 1929). By 1910–1911 *shillabers* and *shills* spanned boxing bulletins ([*Grand Forks Evening Times*, 12 September 1910, p. 3](https://tile.loc.gov/storage-services/service/ndnp/ndhi/batch_ndhi_deering_ver01/data/sn85042373/00212475865/1910091201/0604.xml), `CERT-1400-209`), Pinkerton crime essays ([*Leslie's Weekly*, 13 October 1910, p. 374](https://archive.org/details/sim_leslies-weekly_1910-10-13_111_2875/page/n9/mode/1up), `CERT-1400-210`), Chicago sports verse ([*Chicago Sunday Tribune*, 20 November 1910](https://archive.org/details/per_chicago-daily-tribune_1910-11-20_69_47/page/n26/mode/1up), `CERT-1400-211`), Coney Island Dreamland reports ([*Toronto Saturday Night* ex *NY Evening Sun*, 3 June 1911, p. 10](https://archive.org/details/sim_saturday-night_1911-06-03_24_34/page/n9/mode/1up), `CERT-1400-212`), Milwaukee street-carnival exposés equating `Shillabers, or "shills"` ([*Social-Democratic Herald*, 24 June & 23 September 1911, p. 8](https://archive.org/details/Social_Democratic_Herald_1911-09-23/page/n7/mode/1up), `CERT-1200-111`, `CERT-1400-213`), and Chicago street-faker *shilliber* in [*Variety* (December 1911, p. 23)](https://archive.org/details/variety25-1911-12/page/n128/mode/1up) (`CERT-1200-112`).

Full-page scans also solve both metropolitan syndicates that introduced nominal *shill*—explicitly inside **auction rooms**—to general readers:

(Copyright, 1910, by the New York Herald Co.)` (confirmed in [*Printers' Ink*, 9 July 1914, p. 52](https://archive.org/details/sim_marketing-communications-1888_1914-07-09_88_2/page/n51/mode/1up)) and prints both singular `"shill"` and plural `"shills"` 5 years and 1 month before *OED* 1916.
2. **"H. E. Twinells" = Chicago-Born Robert Carlton Brown ("The Fake Auction", October 1910 *Pearson's* -> 2 February 1911 W.N.U. — `CERT-1400-201` to `CERT-1400-205`, `CERT-1200-109`):** Ready-print pages in [*The Mexia Weekly Herald* (2 February 1911, p. 6)](https://texashistory.unt.edu/ark:/67531/metapth302319/m1/6/) (`CERT-1400-203`, pushing `"shill," or "shilliver" in full` 15 days before `CERT-1200-109` and 3 years before 1914), [*The Burnet Bulletin* (9 February 1911, p. 6)](https://texashistory.unt.edu/ark:/67531/metapth1952388/m1/6/) (`CERT-1400-204`, stamped `W. Twinells"` as Chicago-born **Robert Carlton Brown** (1886–1959), proving that both "The Fake Auction" and April 1911 *Metropolitan Magazine*'s "The Watch" stemmed from the same Chicago author's mock-auction reporting.

## 4. German Rotwelsch *Schlepper* (1847–1905) and Bilingual Lexicon Equations

German underworld Rotwelsch ***Schlepper*** matches both the steerer/decoy role and the four-consonant skeleton (`S-L-P-L`) of *shillaber* / *shilliber* / *shilliver*. Phase-2 recovery pushes underworld *Schlepper* back fifteen years before F. C. B. Avé-Lallemant (1862)—and **thirty-six years before 1883 *shillaber***—to Berlin police official C. W. Zimmermann's [*Die Diebe in Berlin* (1847, p. 162)](https://archive.org/details/10621838bsb/page/n167/mode/1up) (`CERT-1400-302`; reprinted in Friedrich Kluge's [*Rotwelsch*, Vol. 1, 1901, p. 387](https://archive.org/details/rotwelschquelle00kluggoog/page/n405/mode/1up), `CERT-1400-101`):

> Schleppen, den Spielern und Betrügern die Freier zuführen, daher Schlepper, m., ein Mensch, der gegen Belohnung von Seiten der Spieler u. s. w. hiervon ein Gewerbe macht.

Avé-Lallemant's [*Das deutsche Gaunerthum*, Vol. 4 (1862, pp. 541, 601, 605–606)](https://archive.org/details/dasdeutschegaun01lalgoog/page/n621/mode/1up) (`CERT-1200-301`, upgraded with full stitched pages in `CERT-1400-301` alongside Alexander Harkavy's [1898 6th ed., p. 355](https://archive.org/details/completeenglishj00harkuoft/page/n803/mode/1up); cf. [Hotten 1860, p. 26](https://archive.org/details/bookvagabondsan00hottgoog/page/n73/mode/1up), `CERT-1400-114`, and [Gross 1894, p. 747](https://archive.org/details/11583983bsb/page/n764/mode/1up)) pairs *Schlepper* directly with fairground and tavern card-sharps (*Freischupper*). In America and in bilingual dictionaries between 1874 and 1905, *Schlepper* translated the exact English underworld terms co-occurring with *shillaber*:

* **1874 U.S. German Press (`CERT-1400-102`):** Harrisburg's [*Pennsylvanische Staats-Zeitung* (10 September 1874, p. 2)](https://archive.org/details/per_pennsylvanische-staats-zeitung_pennsylvanische-staats-zeitung_1874-09-10_1/page/n1/mode/1up) reports the arrest of bunco `"Schlepper, Helfershelfer und Macher"` targeting country livestock dealers.
* **1884 & 1891–1897 Bilingual Lexicons (`CERT-1400-103` to `CERT-1400-105`):** Armin Tenner ([1884, p. 94](https://archive.org/details/amerika01tenn/page/n595/mode/2up)) glosses American `"Runner ... etwa: Schlepper"`; Eduard Muret's [*Encyklopädisches Wörterbuch* (1891, Vol. I.1, p. 392)](https://archive.org/details/encyclopaedic01pt1mureuoft/page/n423/mode/2up) unites gaming-house *Schlepper* (2a) and hired auction bidder (2b) under English ***capper*** (`2. a) Helfershelfer m, Schlepper m (Spielhölle); b) Treiber m, gedungener Bieter (Versteigerung)`); and Muret-Sanders ([c. 1897, leaf n629](https://archive.org/details/encyklopdisches00muregoog/page/n629/mode/1up)) equates `roper-in Am.` with `Lockvogel m, Schlepper m`.
* **1895 & 1905 Chicago *Abendpost* & Negative Control (`CERT-1400-106` to `CERT-1400-108`):** Downtown Chicago's [*Abendpost*](https://archive.org/details/sim_abendpost-sonntagpost_1895-03-23_7_70/page/n1/mode/1up) called Wabash Avenue confidence steerers `Schlepper` on 23 March 1895 (p. 2) and glossed licensed downtown storefront touts on [9 October 1905 (p. 5)](https://archive.org/details/sim_abendpost-sonntagpost_1905-10-09_17_238/page/n4/mode/1up) as `Schlepper („Runner“)`. Yet German accounts of American mock auctions ([Moritz Busch 1854, Vol. 1, pp. 100–102](https://archive.org/details/rcin.org.pl.WA51_86779_PTG12133-1-r1854_Wanderungen-t1_67435/page/n111/mode/1up)) used borrowed `"Peter Funk"`, never `*Schillaber`—confirming that *shillaber* was coined on the English-speaking side of bilingual auction rooms.

## 5. Phonotactic Adaptation (`#ʃl-`) and Abrupt Mock-Surname Remodeling

Phase-2 testing refutes naive mechanical `#ʃl-` -> `shil-` vowel epenthesis. Written English borrowed [*schlemiel*](https://www.oed.com/dictionary/schlemiel_n) (1868) and [*schlepper*](https://www.oed.com/dictionary/schlepper_n) ([*Griffin Daily News*, 6 October 1894, p. 3](https://gahistoricnewspapers.galileo.usg.edu/lccn/sn89053183/1894-10-06/ed-1/seq-3/), `CERT-1400-115`; [*Jewish Chronicle*, 7 June 1901, p. 13](https://archive.org/details/sim_jewish-chronicle_the-jewish-chronicle_1901-06-07_1679/page/n12/mode/1up), `CERT-1400-116`) with intact `schl-`. Auditing ten American Stage-German dialect books (1869–1904, including [Dick 1879, p. 125](https://archive.org/details/dicksdutchfrench00dickiala/page/n128/mode/1up), `CERT-1400-303`) yielded **127 intact `#shl-` / `#schl-` tokens** and **zero epenthetic `#shVl-` tokens**, while [*The New York Times* (25 May 1898, p. 6)](https://archive.org/details/sim_new-york-times_1898-05-25_47_15092/page/n6/mode/1up) confirms Anglophones adapted `/ʃl-/` to `/sl-/` or `/ʃl-/` (`Schley` -> `"Sly"` / `"Shly"`, never `*Shilley`; cf. [Davidson, *Phonology* 23, 2006](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/phonology/article/explaining-sonority-projection-effects/F49FB688F2BD29E9DCEDF0432D1CD802)), and 1850–1910 newsprint contains zero genuine `*shillapper` spellings (`CERT-1400-216`).

Instead, like Lake Michigan's *Shillagalee Point* (`<` *Skillagalee* `<` *Île aux Galets*, [*Chicago Tribune*, 2 September 1889, p. 8](https://archive.org/details/per_chicago-daily-tribune_the-chicago-tribune_1889-09-02_49/page/n7/mode/1up), `CERT-1400-303`; cf. `CERT-1200-303`) and slang personal-name remodelings documented in A. Smythe Palmer ([1882, pp. 29, 96](https://archive.org/details/cu31924027422405/page/n127/mode/1up)) and Josef Reinius ([1903, pp. 63, 74](https://archive.org/details/ontransferredap02reingoog/page/n92/mode/1up), `CERT-1400-304` — *billy* `<` *billon*, *Jemmy-John* `<` *damagan*, *smart Aleck*), *Schlepper* underwent **abrupt mock-surname remodeling**. In Chicago mock-auction rooms where sham bidders had been styled ***Peter Funk*** since Asa Greene ([1834, p. 52](https://archive.org/details/perilsofpearlst00gree/page/52/mode/1up), `CERT-1200-211`) and Matsell ([1859, pp. 39, 114](https://archive.org/details/cu31924073798740/page/n10/mode/2up), `CERT-1200-212`), English-speaking cappers mapped `/ˈʃlɛpər/` directly onto humorist Benjamin Penhallow Shillaber's household surname ***Shillaber*** (routinely spelled *Shilaber* and *Shilliber* in period press, `CERT-1200-204`, `CERT-1200-209`), simultaneously resolving initial `/ʃl-/` and supplying `-aber` (`shillaber` 1883 -> `shilliver` / `shilliber` 1911 -> `shill` 1910).
