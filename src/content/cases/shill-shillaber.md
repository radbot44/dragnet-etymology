## Why *Shillaber* Looked Like a Twentieth-Century Carnival Mystery

In the [*Oxford English Dictionary*](https://www.oed.com/dictionary/shillaber_n), *shillaber* ("of unknown origin") begins on 6 December 1913 in *Collier's*, followed by the verb [*shill*](https://www.oed.com/dictionary/shill_v3) in 1914 and the noun [*shill*](https://www.oed.com/dictionary/shill_n) in 1916. [*Merriam-Webster*](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/shill) similarly lists c. 1914 for the verb and c. 1916 for the noun, noting that *shill* is *"thought to be a shortened form of the older synonymous term shillaber, but the etymological trail goes cold there."*

Two crucial pieces of earlier detective work narrowed the gap without explaining where the word began. Researcher Sven Yargs (documented on [English Language & Usage StackExchange Q62859](https://english.stackexchange.com/questions/62859/origin-of-the-word-shill-shillaber) and incorporated into Douglas Harper's [*Online Etymology Dictionary*](https://www.etymonline.com/word/shill)) pushed *shillaber* back to a 15 February 1908 *Los Angeles Herald* cane-and-ring game report and *shill* to April 1911 (*The Metropolitan Magazine*). Even further back, Jonathon Green's [*Green's Dictionary of Slang*](https://greensdictofslang.com/entry/jm5glaq) cited a lone sentence from a small western Pennsylvania weekly—the *Indiana Weekly Messenger* of 14 March 1883—before jumping thirty-one years to 1914. Because that rural Pennsylvania snippet carried no origin credit, scholars continued to treat *shillaber* as turn-of-the-century circus or carnival slang.

## Tracing the 1883 Trail Back to Clark and State Streets, Chicago

Searching digitized regional newspapers for neighboring phrases from *Green's* 1883 sentence recovers the complete two-column investigative exposé forty-nine days earlier in [*The Janesville Daily Gazette* (24 January 1883, p. 3, cols. 2–3)](https://archive.org/details/janesville-daily-gazette-1883-01-24/page/n2/mode/1up). At the bottom of column 3 sits the credit line missing from the later Pennsylvania reprint: **`—Chicago Times.`**

Across fourteen printed occurrences of *shillaber* and *shillabers* (plus one compositor slip, *shallabers*), the *Chicago Times* reporter described downtown watch-and-jewelry "red-flag" auction storefronts packed with thieves' cant (*s. g. watch* for silver-gilt watch, *yellow super* for gold watch, *capping*, and *guys* for marks):

> MOCK AUCTIONS. There is a red flag at the door. The room is always small, with an office at one end where the swindled purchaser pays for what he buys, and so situated as to protect the shillaber in his sham purchase or the victim from the gaze of the crowd, for it is not prudent to have two "guys" at a time at the cashier's desk.

Less than five months later, an independent municipal police report in [*The Chicago Daily Tribune* (7 June 1883, p. 8, col. 3)](https://archive.org/details/per_chicago-daily-tribune_the-chicago-daily-tribun_1883-06-07_42/page/n7/mode/1up) confirmed that *shillaber* was active Chicago street terminology, naming two mock-auction storefronts preying on visitors in town for the National Exposition of Railway Appliances and the circus:

> The proprietors of the shops at No. 110 Clark street and No. 300 State street have both been under arrest during the last month on complaint of strangers who have been roped-in and robbed by the clarion-voiced auctioneer and his gang of "shillabers."

By contrast, searching 513 digitized circus route-book personnel rosters (1872–1912) in the [Illinois State University Milner Library Circus Route Books Collection](https://digital.library.illinoisstate.edu/digital/collection/p15990coll5/id/11411/) recovers zero occurrences of *shillaber* or *shill* (which consistently use *ballyhoo* and *booster*). Even twenty-nine years after 1883, when the 12 May 1912 *Chicago Sunday Tribune* explained clipped *shills* to general readers, it still placed them in fake auction rooms on **South State Street, Chicago**.

## How *Shillaber* Clipped to *Shill* (1908–1911)

Once urban pitchmen rotated between winter mock-auction storefronts and summer street fairs, medicine shows, and sideshow midways, *shillaber* surfaced in show-trade weeklies—beginning with [*The Billboard* (7 March 1908, p. 24)](https://archive.org/details/sim_billboard_1908-03-07_20_10/page/n23/mode/1up), [*The Film Index* (3 April 1909, p. 7)](https://archive.org/details/filmindex190907film/page/n201/mode/1up), and Walter Prichard Eaton's January 1910 essay in [*The Green Book Album* (p. 66)](https://archive.org/details/sim_green-book-magazine_1910-01_3_1/page/n66/mode/1up).

Newly recovered magazine and newspaper scans from 1910–1911 push every clipped form earlier than current dictionaries—and show verbal clipping appearing in print six months ahead of the clipped noun:

## Evaluating the Contact Etymology: Rotwelsch *Schlepper* + American *Shillaber*

Restoring the word's earliest documented habitat to 1880s downtown Chicago mock-auction rooms brings a strong underworld contact candidate into focus. In Friedrich Christian Benedict Avé-Lallemant's four-volume study of German thieves' cant, [*Das deutsche Gaunerthum* (Vol. 4, 1862, p. 601)](https://archive.org/details/dasdeutschegaun01lalgoog/page/n616/mode/1up), **Rotwelsch *Schlepper*** denotes the exact role of a confidence-game decoy:

> *Schlepper, synonym mit Fallmacher und Macher, der Gauner, der seinen Genossen die Personen anlockt, welche im falschen Spiel betrogen und ausgeplündert werden sollen.* ('the crook who lures victims to his confederates to be cheated and fleeced in a crooked game', paired on p. 605 with fairground card-sharps, *Freischupper*.)

In late nineteenth-century Chicago—where German-speaking immigrants made up over a third of the city's population and bilingual auctioneers worked Clark and State Streets (compare Louis E. Jackson and C. R. Hellyer's [*A Vocabulary of Criminal Slang*, 1914, p. 75](https://archive.org/details/jacksonlouise.hellyerc.r.avocabularyofcriminalslang1914/page/n76/mode/1up), which cross-references *shilliber* directly to cosmopolitan Jewish cant *shoniker*)—English speakers borrowing `/ˈʃlɛpər/` encountered a word-initial `/ʃl-/` cluster absent from native nineteenth-century English phonotactics. Breaking `/ʃl-/` with a short epenthetic vowel (`*[ˈʃɪləpər]`, parallel to U.S. cant *shillagalee* from Dutch *scheeloog* in [Barrère & Leland 1890, Vol. 2, p. 228](https://archive.org/details/slangjargoncant02barriala/page/n239/mode/1up)) and assimilating the unfamiliar word to the household American surname *Shillaber* (routinely spelled *Shilaber* and *Shilliber* in contemporary newspapers, e.g., [*Los Angeles Daily Herald*, 14 May 1875, p. 4](https://archive.org/details/los-angeles-daily-herald-1875-05-14/page/n3/mode/1up)) accounts for the 1883 Chicago locus, the confidence-decoy meaning, and the fluctuating medial consonant across *shillaber*, *shilliber*, and *shilliver*.
