## Pinpointing Jonathon Green's 1883 Lead to Downtown Chicago

Although the [*Oxford English Dictionary*](https://www.oed.com/dictionary/shillaber_n) still opens its unrevised 1986 Supplement entry for *shillaber* ("of unknown origin") on 6 December 1913 in *Collier's* (`CERT-1200-113`), Jonathon Green had already pushed the word's known history back more than thirty years in [*Green's Dictionary of Slang*](https://greensdictofslang.com/entry/jm5glaq), citing a single sentence from a small western Pennsylvania weekly—the *Indiana Weekly Messenger* of 14 March 1883—before a gap to 1914. Researcher Sven Yargs ([English StackExchange Q62859](https://english.stackexchange.com/questions/62859/origin-of-the-word-shill-shillaber), cited in Douglas Harper's [*Online Etymology Dictionary*](https://www.etymonline.com/word/shill)) likewise narrowed the early twentieth-century carnival trail to 15 February 1908 (*Los Angeles Herald*, `CERT-1200-120`) for *shillaber* and April 1911 (*The Metropolitan Magazine*) for clipped *shill*.

Because the 14 March 1883 *Indiana Weekly Messenger* snippet carried no byline or city, it remained unclear where the word was actually being used. Searching nineteenth-century newsprint for adjacent phrases from Green's quotation recovers the full two-column article **49 days earlier** in [*The Janesville Daily Gazette* (24 January 1883, p. 3, cols. 2–3)](https://archive.org/details/janesville-daily-gazette-1883-01-24/page/n2/mode/1up) (`CERT-1200-101`), signed at the foot of column 3 **`—Chicago Times.`** Across fourteen printed tokens of *shillaber* and *shillabers* (plus one compositor slip, *shallabers*), the *Chicago Times* reporter described "red-flag" watch-and-jewelry mock-auction dens using established underworld cant (*s. g. watch* for silver-gilt watch, *yellow super* for gold watch, *capping*, and *guys* for marks):

> MOCK AUCTIONS. There is a red flag at the door. The room is always small, with an office at one end where the swindled purchaser pays for what he buys, and so situated as to protect the shillaber in his sham purchase or the victim from the gaze of the crowd, for it is not prudent to have two "guys" at a time at the cashier's desk.

Less than five months later, an independent local police item in [*The Chicago Daily Tribune* (7 June 1883, p. 8, col. 3)](https://archive.org/details/per_chicago-daily-tribune_the-chicago-daily-tribun_1883-06-07_42/page/n7/mode/1up) (`CERT-1200-102`) confirmed that *shillaber* was active Chicago municipal usage, naming two mock-auction storefronts preying on visitors attending the National Exposition of Railway Appliances:

> The proprietors of the shops at No. 110 Clark street and No. 300 State street have both been under arrest during the last month on complaint of strangers who have been roped-in and robbed by the clarion-voiced auctioneer and his gang of "shillabers."

## Mock-Auction Rooms, Circus Rosters, and the 1910 Clipping to *Shill*

In twentieth-century popular memory, *shillaber* and *shill* were strongly associated with traveling circuses and carnival midways (`CERT-1200-103`, `CERT-1200-105`). To test whether printed circus records preserve the word earlier, we searched 513 digitized circus route-book rosters published between 1872 and 1912 at Illinois State University's Milner Library (`CERT-1200-118`, `CERT-1200-206`), finding zero occurrences of *shillaber* or *shill* (where outside ticket and annex personnel appear under titles such as *Outside Ticket Box* and *ballyhoo*). Corpus absence in printed rosters cannot by itself prove where a spoken slang word originated, but it aligns with the positive 1883 evidence showing *shillaber* entrenched in urban mock-auction rooms twenty-five years before its first known outdoor show-paper appearances in February and March 1908.

Once *shillaber* entered national magazine fiction and show-trade journalism in 1908–1910, clipping to *shill* followed rapidly:

* **Verbal *shill through* and *shilled* (14 May 1910):** George Randolph Chester's circus story "Ballyhoo Bill" in [*The Saturday Evening Post* (14 May 1910, pp. 12, 14)](https://archive.org/details/sim_saturday-evening-post_1910-05-14_182_46/page/12/mode/1up) (`CERT-1200-106`, `CERT-1200-119`, `CERT-1200-215`) prints *"the shillaber brigade"*, *"shill through with the rush"*, and *"Even the mayor shilled in on the free attraction"*—3 years and 7 months before *OED*'s 1914 verb quotation (`CERT-1200-114`).
* **Agentive *Shillers* (9 July 1910) and Simplex Noun *shill* (19 November 1910):** *The Saturday Evening Post* printed *Shillers* on 9 July 1910 (`CERT-1200-107`), and [*The Fort Wayne Sentinel* (19 November 1910, p. 24, col. 3)](https://archive.org/details/fort-wayne-sentinel-1910-11-19/page/n23/mode/1up) (`CERT-1200-108`) explicitly linked the clipped noun back to the auction room—5 months before *Etymonline*'s April 1911 citation and 5 years, 1 month before *OED*'s 1916 noun baseline:

> The auction room is a breeding place for the "plugger," though in its case his technical name is a "shill," and his duty is to lure on to high bids the occasional "sucker" who ventures within.

## Evaluating Proposed Etymologies

Because no nineteenth-century writer explicitly recorded how *shillaber* was coined, any etymology beyond 1883 remains a linguistic hypothesis rather than an established fact. Comparing the surviving candidates against chronology, phonology, and semantics narrows the field considerably:

* **Rotwelsch *Schlepper* + Surname Remodeling (`CERT-1200-301`, `CERT-1200-204` — Most Plausible Reconstruction):** In Friedrich Christian Benedict Avé-Lallemant's [*Das deutsche Gaunerthum* (Vol. 4, 1862, p. 601)](https://archive.org/details/dasdeutschegaun01lalgoog/page/n616/mode/1up), underworld Rotwelsch *Schlepper* (synonymous with *Fallmacher*) denotes *"the crook who lures victims to his confederates to be cheated and fleeced in a crooked game."* In 1870s–1880s Chicago—where German was spoken alongside English across Clark and State Street commercial districts—English speakers adapting `/ˈʃlɛpər/` (breaking the non-native initial `/ʃl-/` cluster with epenthetic `/ɪ/`) had a ready-made folk-etymological template in the household American surname *Shillaber* (famous from humorist B. P. Shillaber's "Mrs. Partington" sketches, `CERT-1200-209`, though Mrs. Partington herself had no decoy role).
* **Previously Suggested Alternatives (Evaluated Hypotheses, Weakened by Chronology or Sense):** Anatoly Liberman (2004) tentatively wondered whether German *Schieber* ('profiteer, crooked operator') might be connected (`CERT-1200-310`), but *Schieber* lacks `/l/` and acquired its black-market profiteer sense during World War I, three decades after 1883 *shillaber*. Suggestions invoking London omnibus pioneer George Shillibeer (`CERT-1200-201`, `CERT-1200-202`, `CERT-1200-203`) face a similar semantic gap: by mid-century *shillibeer* in British slang meant a pauper's funeral hearse (Hotten 1874, p. 91), while London bus touts were called *cads*. Conversely, the 2009 internet claim of a literal Yiddish noun `*shillaber` (`CERT-1200-311`) is an uncorroborated ghost word absent from Alexander Harkavy's 1898 and 1925 Yiddish dictionaries (as James Harbeck noted in 2011 and David L. Gold confirmed in 2020).
