## The Centerpiece Discovery: Boston's 1897 Overhead Line Inventory

Before *gimmick* meant a marketing stunt, a magician's secret prop, or a rigged carnival brake, it was unquoted shop-floor equipment in New England electrical work. Following its earliest known humorous appearance in a 24 February 1893 *Bangor Daily News* sketch about a broken Bucksport, Maine electric-light engine (reprinted verbatim in New York's [*Illustrated Electrical Review*, 11 March 1893, p. 56](https://archive.org/details/sim_factory_1893-03-11_22_3/page/n13/mode/1up), adapting Edgar Wilson "Bill" Nye's 1884 [*Baled Hay*](https://archive.org/details/baledhayadrierb00nyegoog/page/n308/mode/1up) *dewflicker* template), the [*Oxford English Dictionary*](https://www.oed.com/dictionary/gimmick_n) has a thirteen-year gap until 1906.

Auditing all five volumes (2,366 leaves) of Boston's *Inventory of Machinery, Tools, Furniture, Fixtures, Horses, Harness and Miscellaneous Equipment of the West End St. Ry. Co., Sept. 30, 1897* closes that gap with four unquoted administrative entries in [Volume 4](https://archive.org/details/inventoryofweste04west/page/n112/mode/1up)—including one on page 62 disguised in raw OCR as `2 Gininiicks`:

Across the seven emergency line-repair districts, *Gimmick* and *Comealong* stand in complementary distribution. In electrical supply catalogs of the 1880s and 1890s—such as [*Partrick & Carter's 1888 Catalogue* (p. 30)](https://archive.org/details/PartrickAndCarter1888Catalogue/page/n33/mode/1up) and Boston Thompson-Brown Electric Co.'s `T.-B. COME-ALONG` ([*Street Railway Review*, 15 Feb. 1895, p. 110](https://archive.org/details/streetrailwayrev05amer/page/n121/mode/1up))—a $2.50 *come-along* was a two-jaw hinged or eccentric-cam wire clamp used to grip and tension overhead copper trolley wire. Whether *gimmick* was already a broader New England workshop term that Boston linemen applied to their wire grip (compare [*Telephony*, 7 Feb. 1925, p. 22](https://archive.org/details/sim_telephony_1925-02-07_88_6/page/n11/mode/1up), noting that among pole crews *"the term generally used for any tool or piece of material is gimmick"*) or radiated outward from clamping hardware, its nineteenth-century occupational reality is directly attested.

## Carnival Wheels (1914), Stage Magicians (1923), and Schwartz's *CIGAM* → *GIMAC* (1931–1933)

In a classic *American Speech* note ("Words in Modern Magic", Feb. 1949, pp. 38–42), magician and novelist Albert S. ("Sid") Fleischman observed that *gimmick* was absent from older magic catalogs, argued that conjurers borrowed it from carnival workers, and treated *gimac* as a back-formed anagram of *magic*. Digitized entertainment weeklies and conjuring archives corroborate and sharpen Fleischman's timeline at every step:

- **1914 Carnival Rigging (-9.6 years vs. *OED3* Sense 1.b):** After zero verified hits across 1,550 weekly issues of *The Billboard*, *New York Clipper*, *Variety*, and *Show World* from 1905–1913, *gimmick* appears in [*New York Clipper* (10 Jan. 1914, p. 5)](https://archive.org/details/per_new-york-clipper_new-york-clipper_1914-01-10_61_48) and in Chicago supplier **H. C. Evans & Co.**'s rigged paddle-wheel advertisements ([*The Billboard*, 21 Mar. 1914, p. 14](https://archive.org/details/sim_billboard_1914-03-21_26_12); [*New York Clipper*, 4 Apr. 1914, p. 23](https://archive.org/details/per_new-york-clipper_the-new-york-clipper_1914-04-04_62_8)), pricing the wheel `$75.00` complete or `$35.00` *"WITHOUT Gimmick."* [*Variety* (2 June 1922, p. 8)](https://archive.org/details/sim_variety_1922-06-02_67_2) later glossed the carnival control brake as *"the squeeze," "the pinch" or "the gimmick."*
- **December 1923 Stage-Magic Adoption (-8.7 years vs. *OED3*):** Across 20.53 million characters of searched UK and U.S. conjuring books, dealer catalogs, and periodicals prior to December 1923 (including *Mahatma*, Houdini's [*Conjurers' Monthly*](https://archive.org/details/conjurersmonthly00unse), [*The Magic Wand*](https://archive.org/details/the-magic-wand-vol-i-september-1910-to-august-1911), and Thayer's [*Magical Bulletin*](https://archive.org/details/magical-bulletin-of-the-magical-shop-of-the-west-january-1915-december-1917); note that *The Sphinx* remains commercially paywalled outside open archives), no verified occurrences of *gimmick* or *gimac* were recovered—magicians wrote *feke* or *fake*. In [*The Billboard* (1 Dec. 1923, p. 53)](https://archive.org/details/sim_billboard_1923-12-01_35_14), magic reviewers marked the newcomer in quotes: *"In the parlance and argot of the present-day tendency, the tricks were a fine collection of 'gimmicks' on exhibition."*
- **1931 *CIGAM* and 1933 *GIMAC*:** Digitized booklets in the State Library Victoria's Alma Conjuring Collection show Connecticut magician **Walter A. Schwartz** publishing [*CIGAM* (1931)](https://viewer.slv.vic.gov.au/?entity=IE6892863) (*MAGIC* backwards) with the cover acrostic **`CONJURING ILLUSIONS GIMMICKS ART MYSTERY`**, followed two years later by his anagrammatically titled sequel [*GIMAC* (1933)](https://viewer.slv.vic.gov.au/?entity=IE6901991) (crediting magicians Johnny Jones and Robert C. Anderson for suggesting the title while still heading page 8 `A NEW GIMMICK`)—three years before Isidor Colodny's *Words* (Nov. 1936) popularized the *gimac* < *magic* origin myth.

## Reconsidering *Gimmal* and *Gimmer*: Competing Nineteenth-Century Pronunciations

Eric Partridge ([*Origins*, 1958](https://archive.org/details/originsshortetym0000part)) suggested connecting *gimmick* to *gimbal* (perhaps influenced by *gimcrack* or *trick*), whereas *OED3* noted that historical British pronunciations of *gimcrack*, *gimmal*, and mechanical *gimmer* ('two-part hinge, quaint piece of machinery') used soft `/dʒ/` rather than hard `/ɡ/`.

Inspecting fourteen British and American dictionary editions between 1828 and 1901 reveals **competing pronunciation traditions** rather than uniform `/dʒ/`. While Worcester ([1860, p. 617](https://archive.org/details/cu31924027443393/page/n692/mode/1up)), the *Century Dictionary* ([1889, p. 2513](https://archive.org/details/centurydictipt900whituoft/page/n104/mode/1up)), and Oxford's *New English Dictionary* ([*OED1*, 1901, pp. 166–67](https://archive.org/details/oed04arch/page/n807/mode/1up)—which still gave soft `(dʒi·mbăl)` even for *gimbal*!) recorded `/dʒ/`, the entire Merriam-Webster lineage from [Noah Webster (1828)](https://archive.org/details/americandictiona01websrich/page/n830/mode/1up) and [Porter & Wheeler (1865, p. 570: `Ḡĭm'mal`, `Ḡĭm'mer`)](https://archive.org/details/americandictiona00websuoft/page/n647/mode/1up) through the freshly reset [*Webster's International Dictionary* (1890/1898, p. 625)](https://archive.org/details/webstersinternat00port/page/n742/mode/1up)—joined by Ogilvie & Annandale's [*Imperial Dictionary* (1882, Vol. 2, p. 387)](https://archive.org/details/bwb_KU-191-321_2/page/n396/mode/1up) and Funk & Wagnalls' [*Standard Dictionary* (1893, Vol. 1, p. 763)](https://archive.org/details/standarddictiona01funkuoft/page/n802/mode/1up)—recognized hard `/ɡ/` on both *gimmal* and *gimmer* well before *gimmick* surfaced in print. Combined with attested dialectal tool forms such as *gimlick* for *gimlet* ([*EDD*, Vol. 2, p. 615](https://archive.org/details/englishdialectdi02wrig/page/n626/mode/1up)) and *maddick* for *mattock* ([*EDD*, Vol. 4, p. 4](https://archive.org/details/englishdialectdi04wrig/page/n15/mode/1up)), nineteenth-century hard-`/ɡ/` *gimmer*/*gimmal* reopens a plausible English workshop ancestry alongside *gimcrack*.
