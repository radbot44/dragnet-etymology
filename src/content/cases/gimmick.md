## Uniting the 1893 Bucksport Baselines and Documenting the 1897 Boston Tool Inventory

Two reference monuments open the history of *gimmick* in early 1893: the [*Oxford English Dictionary*](https://www.oed.com/dictionary/gimmick_n) quotes the *Bangor (Maine) Daily News* of **24 February 1893** (p. 5, col. 2), while [*Green's Dictionary of Slang*](https://greensdictofslang.com/entry/uewciia) and J. E. Lighter's *Historical Dictionary of American Slang* cite New York's *Electrical Review* of **11 March 1893** (p. 56, col. 2). Inspecting the [*Illustrated Electrical Review* scan](https://archive.org/details/sim_factory_1893-03-11_22_3/page/n13/mode/1up) (`CERT-900-101`) confirms that these are the exact same Bucksport, Maine electric-light plant item, explicitly credited at the foot to **`—Bangor, Me., News.`**:

> Dark Times in Bucksport. The electric light plant at Bucksport is busted and the town has gone back to kerosene till the engine can be mended. A few minutes after starting up last night the engine was taken with a series of spasms, the parabola of the dewflicker got jammed hard to starboard, which, of course, caused the flopper to fall down on the mizzen reciprocal eccentricity gimmick...—Bangor, Me., News.

Tracing the mock-technical phrase *dewflicker* back to humorist Bill Nye's 1884 collection *Baled Hay* (`CERT-1100-109`, which contains *dewflicker* and *dewdad* but zero occurrences of *gimmick*) shows that the 1893 Maine paragraph grafted an unfamiliar local word—*gimmick*—into an established comic machinery routine.

Four years later—bridging *OED3*'s thirteen-year gap between 1893 and 1906 (`CERT-900-106`)—Volume 4 of Boston's five-volume [*Inventory of the West End Street Railway Company* (dated 30 September 1897)](https://archive.org/details/inventoryofweste04west/page/n112/mode/1up) documents **four unquoted administrative inventory entries** across Boston's overhead trolley-wire crews (`CERT-900-102`, `CERT-900-103`, `CERT-900-104`, and `CERT-1100-101`, the fourth hidden in raw OCR as `2 Gininiicks`):

* **Central Power Station, Line Construction Dept. (Vol. 4, p. 53):** `1 Special Gimmick ( Comealong)` (`CERT-900-102`)
* **Emergency Station District #4 (Vol. 4, p. 61):** `2 Gimmicks` (`CERT-900-103` — earliest known plural, predating *Green's* 1917 plural by 19 years and 7 months)
* **Emergency Station District #5 (Vol. 4, p. 62):** `2 Gimmicks` (`CERT-1100-101`)
* **Emergency Station District #6 (Vol. 4, p. 64):** `1 Wire Gimmick` (`CERT-900-104`)

[Owlmirror previously noted](https://languagehat.com/sebiro/#comment-4611301) the p. 53 and p. 64 entries in a Languagehat comment on 2 September 2024.

Across all seven emergency line districts in Volume 4 (pp. 58–65), *Comealong* (Districts #1, #2, #3, #7) and *Gimmick* (Districts #4, #5, #6) appear in one-to-one complementary distribution, while the central storeroom in Volume 5 (p. 38, `CERT-1100-102`) books the tool under its formal catalog name (`13 Come Alongs Spec. @ 2.50`—a hinged eccentric wire-pulling clamp, `CERT-1100-103`, `CERT-1100-105`, `CERT-1100-106`). Note what this evidence does and does not prove: it establishes that Boston overhead-line crews used *gimmick* as ordinary shop terminology for a come-along clamp by September 1897, without proving that the noun originally denoted *only* that clamp rather than a small mechanical device more generally (compare radiator valves in February 1908, `CERT-900-107`, and telephone-lineman tools broadly by 1925, `CERT-900-109`).

## Carnival Rigging (1914), Stage Magic (1923), and the 1931–1933 *CIGAM* / *GIMAC* Sequel

In a classic February 1949 *American Speech* article ("Words in Modern Magic", 24.1, pp. 38–42), magician and novelist Albert S. ("Sid") Fleischman noticed that pre-1920s magic dealer catalogs lacked *gimmick*, arguing that magicians borrowed the word from carnival slang and that *gimac* was a later playful anagram of *magic*. Primary-source scans across both trades strongly bear out Fleischman's chronology:

* **Carnival Wheels (9 Years, 7 Months Earlier than *OED3* Sense 1.b):** After zero occurrences across 1,550 weekly issues of *The Billboard*, *The New York Clipper*, *Variety*, and *Show World* from 1905 through 1913 (`CERT-1100-205`), carnival print abruptly adopts the word in early 1914—first in *The New York Clipper*'s "Carnival Conversation" column on **10 January 1914** (*'the "gimicks" went wrong'*, `CERT-900-203`), then in classified wheel advertisements in *The Billboard* (**21 March 1914**, `CERT-900-204`) and [*The New York Clipper* (**4 April 1914**, p. 23)](https://archive.org/details/per_new-york-clipper_the-new-york-clipper_1914-04-04_62_8) (`CERT-1100-207`), where Chicago supplier H. C. Evans & Co. priced its 32-inch upright paddle wheel at `$75.00` complete or `$35.00` **`WITHOUT Gimmick`** (a concealed $40 friction control brake, defined in *Variety* on 2 June 1922 as *'"the squeeze," "the pinch" or "the gimmick"'*, `CERT-1100-210`).
* **Stage Conjuring (8 Years, 8 Months Earlier than *OED3*'s Magician Quotation):** Across 20.53 million characters of digitized British and American conjuring periodicals, treatises, and dealer catalogs published before December 1923 (`CERT-900-201`, `CERT-900-202`, `CERT-1100-201` through `CERT-1100-204`, including Harry Houdini's 24-issue *Conjurers' Monthly Magazine*), secret trick devices are consistently called *feke* or *fake*, with zero tokens of *gimmick* or *gimac*. Having spotted carnival *gimmick* in *The Billboard*'s midway gossip on 25 June 1921 (`CERT-1100-209`), conjurers brought it into [*The Billboard*'s magic column on **1 December 1923** (p. 53, col. 1)](https://archive.org/details/sim_billboard_1923-12-01_35_14) (`CERT-900-209`), still framed in scare quotes as a recent borrowing: *"In the parlance and argot of the present-day tendency, the tricks were a fine collection of 'gimmicks' on exhibition."*
* **How *CIGAM* (1931) Produced *GIMAC* (1933):** Digitized copies in State Library Victoria's Alma Conjuring Collection pinpoint the exact printed source behind the 1936 *Words* anagram note cited in *OED3*. In 1931, Connecticut magician Walter A. Schwartz published [*CIGAM* (`MAGIC` reversed)](https://viewer.slv.vic.gov.au/?entity=IE6892863) (`CERT-1100-211`), expanding the cover acrostic as **`CONJURING ILLUSIONS GIMMICKS ART MYSTERY`**. Two years later, needing a second five-letter permutation of `MAGIC` for his 1933 sequel, Schwartz adopted [*GIMAC*](https://viewer.slv.vic.gov.au/?entity=IE6901991) (`CERT-900-212`, `CERT-1100-306`, suggested by magicians "Little" Johnny Jones and Robert C. Anderson, `CERT-1100-212`, `CERT-1100-213`), while still titling a trick on page 8 **`A NEW GIMMICK`**. Three years later, an unsigned squib in Isidor Colodny's Los Angeles monthly *Words* (November 1936, `CERT-1100-214`) mistook that 1933 book-title pun for the origin of *gimmick*.

## Evaluating the Workshop Pronunciation & Origin Hypothesis

Eric Partridge (*Origins*, 1958) suggested connecting *gimmick* with the mechanical *gimbal / gimmal / gimcrack* family, an idea modern dictionaries have generally doubted because *gimcrack*—and British dictionary entries for *gimmal* and *gimmer* ('a two-part hinge; a quaint piece of machinery')—have soft `/dʒ/` (`j`) rather than hard `/ɡ/`.

Checking fourteen British and American dictionary editions published between 1828 and 1901 (`CERT-900-302`, `CERT-900-304`, `CERT-1100-301` through `CERT-1100-303`) reveals a split pronunciation tradition: while Worcester (1860), the *Century Dictionary* (1889), and *OED1* (1901—which still gave `(dʒi·mbăl)` even for *gimbal*!) recorded soft `/dʒ/`, **every unabridged Merriam-Webster edition from Noah Webster's 1828 *American Dictionary* through Noah Porter's freshly reset 1890 *Webster's International Dictionary*** (alongside Annandale's 1882 *Imperial* and Funk & Wagnalls's 1893 *Standard*) explicitly marked mechanical **`Ḡĭm'mal`** and **`Ḡĭm'mer`** ('a mechanical device or contrivance; a gimcrack') with hard velar `/ɡ/`, by the same leveling that gave modern English hard-`/ɡ/` *gimbal*. While direct pre-1893 shop-floor citations of mechanical *gimmer* or *gimmal* remain an open question, the nineteenth-century American dictionary record removes the main phonological barrier to a domestic workshop formation (alongside Northern British tool-noun *gimlick* 'gimlet', `CERT-900-301`, or blending with *gimcrack*, `CERT-1100-304`), whereas foreign lookalikes such as German *Gemach* ('room, privy', `CERT-900-306`) and Dutch *gemak* ('ease, privy', `CERT-900-308`) diverge sharply in both stress and meaning.
