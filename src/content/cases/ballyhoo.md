## 1. Lexicographical Baselines and the Two-*Ballyhoo* Problem

Few words in English bridge two more distinct occupational worlds than *ballyhoo*. In nineteenth-century Atlantic seamanship, *ballahoo* (also spelled *ballahou*, *balahoo*, and *ballyhoo*) designated a fast, sharp-floored West Indian two-masted fore-and-aft schooner with raking masts and no square topsails—and subsequently, among deep-water square-rig sailors, any crank, slovenly, or ill-conditioned vessel. By 1900, *ballyhoo* had become the watchword of the American carnival midway and circus sideshow, naming first the noisy free performance staged on an elevated platform outside a tent, then the outside talker ("barker") who worked that platform, and finally sensationalized commercial or political hype.

Reference dictionaries still treat those two traditions as separate homographs lacking documentary connection:

1. **Nautical *ballahoo, n.* and *ballyhoo, n.¹*:** While [Douglas Harper's *Etymonline* (`ballyhoo`)](https://www.etymonline.com/word/ballyhoo) and [*OED Online* (`ballyhoo, n.¹`, 1836–1897)](https://www.oed.com/dictionary/ballyhoo_n1) retain the 1836 literary baseline from Michael Scott's *The Cruise of the Midge*, the revised entry at [*OED Online* (`ballahoo, n.`, 1815–)](https://www.oed.com/dictionary/ballahoo_n) pushed generic English usage back to **1815** in *The Times* (London), deriving the noun from Caribbean Spanish *balahú* and West Indian French *balaou*. Herman Melville's forecastle oath *"this ballyhoo of blazes"* in *Omoo* (1847) stands at [*OED Online* (`ballyhoo of blazes, n.`)](https://www.oed.com/dictionary/ballyhoo-of-blazes_n).
2. **Colloquial and Midway *ballyhoo, n.²*, *ballyhoo, v.*, and Clipped *bally*:** Jonathan E. Lighter's [*Historical Dictionary of American Slang*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Historical_Dictionary_of_American_Slang) (Vol. 1, 1994), Jonathon Green's [*Green's Dictionary of Slang* (`ballyhoo, n.`)](https://greensdictofslang.com/entry/47m74hq), and *Etymonline* open the carnival sense in **August 1901** with Mary Bronson Hartt's Buffalo Pan-American Exposition essay in *The World's Work* (`CERT-1800-113`). *OED Online*'s September 2024 revision of [`ballyhoo, n.²`](https://www.oed.com/dictionary/ballyhoo_n2) improved the nineteenth-century record—dating Sense 1 (euphemism for *hell* in *give old ballyhoo*) to **25 September 1858**, Sense 2 ('noisy fuss, commotion; overblown publicity') to **10 November 1868** (with a 38-year citation gap between 1873 and 1911), and Sense 3 ('free outside carnival show, barker, or pitch') to **19 November 1895** in *The Atlanta Journal*. Yet *OED3* still dates [`ballyhoo, v.`](https://www.oed.com/dictionary/ballyhoo_v) to **1911** (vs. **4 November 1903** in [Green's `ballyhoo, v.`](https://greensdictofslang.com/entry/tzswf3a)), [`ballyhooing, n.`](https://www.oed.com/dictionary/ballyhooing_n) to **1902**, and [`ballyhooed, adj.`](https://www.oed.com/dictionary/ballyhooed_adj) to **1919**, while omitting carnival back-clipped *bally, n.* (**1909** in Lighter; **24 February 1929** in [Green's `bally, n.¹`](https://greensdictofslang.com/entry/faz6cwy)) and *bally, v.* (**April 1942** in [Green's `bally, v.`](https://greensdictofslang.com/entry/5x3wj5q)).

In *American Speech* (20 [1945]: 184–186; 40 [1965]: 32–39), Atcheson L. Hench proposed that terrestrial *ballyhoo* grew out of maritime *ballahoo* / *ballyhoo* via sailors' *ballyhoo of blazes*. *OED3* (`ballyhoo, n.²`) judged that *"there appears to be no evidence to link"* the two nouns, while H. L. Mencken's [*The American Language* (4th ed., 1936, pp. 188, 568, 584)](https://archive.org/details/in.ernet.dli.2015.169487) repeated a showman story tracing *ballyhoo* to Arabic street cries at the 1893 Chicago World's Fair. Primary-source scans across Caribbean lexicons, Atlantic shipping columns, regional newspapers, and over 65.7 million characters of digitized amusement weeklies allow every link to be tested directly.

## 2. Caribbean Ichthyonym to Anglo-American Schooner (1665–1834)

The maritime noun began in the Lesser Antilles as an indigenous Kalinago fish name transferred metaphorically to a slender local hull. In Raymond Breton's [*Dictionaire caraïbe-françois* (1665, p. 69)](https://archive.org/details/dictionairecarai00bret) (`CERT-1800-001`), Island Carib *Bálaoû* is glossed *"poisson, dit éguille de mer"*. Père Jean-Baptiste Labat's [*Nouveau voyage aux isles de l'Amérique* (1722, Vol. 6, pp. 400–401)](https://archive.org/details/nouveauvoyageaux06laba_0) (`CERT-1800-002`) describes the Antillean *Balaou*—the ballyhoo halfbeak, *Hemiramphus brasiliensis*—highlighting its projecting lower jaw (*avant-bec*), shaped like a sharp-prowed Caribbees boat running with a long bowsprit. By the late eighteenth century, French Antillean *balaou* denoted an armed, narrow-hulled schooner, printed in Philadelphia by Moreau de Saint-Méry in [*Description... de l'isle Saint-Domingue* (Tome II, 1798, p. 507)](https://archive.org/details/descriptiontopog01more) (`CERT-1800-003`: *"un petit balaou anglais de 6 canons de 4"*) and recorded in Auguste Jal's [*Glossaire nautique* (1848, p. 219)](https://archive.org/details/bub_gb_xZw_AAAAcAAJ/page/n223/mode/1up) (`CERT-1800-006`).

French *balaou* alone does not account for English medial *-h-* (*ballahoo*, *ballahou*). That consonant reflects Caribbean Spanish hiatus-breaking aspiration: the Madrid [*Diccionario marítimo español* (1831, p. 79)](https://archive.org/details/diccionariomarit00madruoft) (`CERT-1800-004`) equates Antillean *BALAHÚ* with French *Balaou* and notes, *"aspirando la h, es muy comun el pronunciar balajú"*. Conversely, Esteban Pichardo's [*Diccionario provincial de voces cubanas* (1836, p. 25)](https://archive.org/details/diccionarioprovi00pich) (`CERT-1800-005`) omits *balahú* entirely between *BAJONAO* and *BALANZA*, localizing Anglo-American contact to the Eastern Caribbean, Puerto Rico, Saint-Domingue, Demerara, and Bermuda.

Separating proper ship names from generic nouns clarifies the English chronology:

* **Onomastic Precursors (1796–1834):** Philadelphia marine lists in the [*Gazette of the United States* (21 June 1796, p. 3, col. 4)](https://www.loc.gov/resource/sn84026273/1796-06-21/ed-1/?sp=3) (`CERT-1800-007`) log `"Schr. Ballahoo, Peterſon, Demarara 14"`, condemned at Port-de-Paix on [25 October 1797 (p. 3)](https://panewsarchive.psu.edu/lccn/sn83025881/1797-10-25/ed-1/seq-3/) (`CERT-1800-008`). In 1804 the Royal Navy built eighteen Bermuda-cedar 4-gun schooners led by HMS *Ballahoo* (spelled *Balahou* in [*The Naval Chronicle*, Vol. 21, 1809, p. 106](https://archive.org/details/navalchronicleco21londiala), `CERT-1800-009`, and *Balaboo* upon her capture by the Baltimore privateer *Perry* in [*Niles' Weekly Register*, 30 July 1814, Vol. 6, p. 373](https://archive.org/details/nilesweeklyregis6181balt), `CERT-1800-012`), alongside Napoleon's *Balaou, No. 5* in the [*Augusta Chronicle* (12 June 1812, p. 2)](https://gahistoricnewspapers.galileo.usg.edu/lccn/sn82014185/1812-06-12/ed-1/seq-2/) (`CERT-1800-011`). Even the magazine serialization of Scott's *The Cruise of the Midge* in [*Blackwood's Edinburgh Magazine* (December 1834, Vol. 36, p. 812)](https://archive.org/details/sim_blackwoods-magazine_1834-12_36_229/page/n103/mode/1up) (`CERT-1800-014`) prints only the capitalized brig name *BALLAHOO*.
* **Generic Antedatings (1810, 1815, and 1826):** Five years before *OED3*'s 1815 London *Times* baseline—and 26 years before the 1836 book edition of *The Cruise of the Midge*—Puerto Rico shipping news in Savannah's [*The Republican; and Savannah Evening Ledger* (16 June 1810, p. 3, col. 3)](https://gahistoricnewspapers.galileo.usg.edu/lccn/sn82014388/1810-06-16/ed-1/seq-3/) (`CERT-1800-010`) prints a lowercase attributive compound specifying the diagnostic rig later defined by W. H. Smyth's [*The Sailor's Word-Book* (1867, p. 74)](https://archive.org/details/sailorswordbook00smytgoog) (`CERT-1800-018`) and W. Clark Russell's [*Sailors' Language* (1883, p. 13)](https://archive.org/details/sailorslanguage00russgoog) (`CERT-1800-020`):

> The mate and two sea-men (the only persons on boaad) taking advantage of the master and supercargo's absence, who were on shore on business, ranaway with the vessel. *The Sukey is a ballahou built schooner, no top-sails, and has a white bottom.*

Commodore Daniel T. Patterson's Barataria dispatch in [*Niles' Weekly Register* (7 January 1815, Vol. 7, p. 293)](https://archive.org/details/nilesweeklyregis7181balt) (`CERT-1800-013`) adds `"a balahoo schooner"`, followed in Britain by `"a beautiful ballahou-schooner"` in William James's [*The Naval History of Great Britain* (Vol. 3, 1826, p. 291)](https://archive.org/details/navalhistorygre04jamegoog) (`CERT-1800-015`).

## 3. Naval Pejoration and the 1877–1889 *Ballahoo* Slang Bridge

William James's [*Naval History* (1826, Vol. 3, p. 553)](https://archive.org/details/navalhistorygre04jamegoog) (`CERT-1800-015`) explains why a fast West Indian schooner became a byword for an awkward vessel: the Admiralty's tiny 55-foot Bermuda *Ballahoo*-class schooners *"raised a laugh at the expense of the projector"* and *"proved so crank and unseaworthy, that almost every one of them that escaped capture went to the bottom."* Combined with square-riggers' disdain for topsail-less fore-and-aft craft, *ballahoo* entered forecastle speech for a rolling death-trap.

In Herman Melville's [*Omoo* (1847, p. 295)](https://archive.org/details/melvilleomoo/page/n313/mode/1up) (`CERT-1800-016`), that pejorative noun crosses two thresholds when `"a curly-pated little Belfast sailor"` warns against shipping aboard `"this ballyhoo of blazes"`. First, the Ulster speaker reduces medial *-a-* to *-y-*, assimilating *ballahoo* to Irish *Bally-* and Anglo-Irish *Ballyhooly* ('faction-fight thrashing', attested in [*Tait's Edinburgh Magazine*, August 1837, p. 526](https://archive.org/details/sim_taits-edinburgh-magazine_1837-08_4_44/page/n57/mode/1up), `CERT-1800-205`). Second, the alliterative tag *of blazes* tied *ballyhoo* to euphemisms for *hell*—yielding 1858 *give old ballyhoo* as well as `"catching ballyhoo"` in [*The Galveston Daily News* (7 May 1889, p. 4)](https://texashistory.unt.edu/ark:/67531/metapth467349/m1/4/) (`CERT-1800-022`, 29 years before *OED3* 1918) and `"knocked to Ballyhoo"` in the [*Pittsburg Dispatch* (13 May 1889, p. 4)](https://panewsarchive.psu.edu/lccn/sn84024546/1889-05-13/ed-1/seq-4/) (`CERT-1800-023`, 16 years before *OED3* 1905).

Four verified newsprint citations supply the missing orthographic proof linking maritime *ballahoo* to terrestrial American *ballyhoo*:

* **2 March 1877 (*The Daily Constitution*, Atlanta, GA, p. 1, col. 4, `CERT-1800-208`):** During a raucous congressional convention nine years before Robert Martin's 1886 London song, a delegate reports `"that THE GRAND BALLYHOO was at hand"`, followed in [*Harper's New Monthly Magazine* (July 1880, p. 216)](https://archive.org/details/sim_harpers-magazine_1880-07_61_362/page/n55/mode/1up) (`CERT-1800-101`) by a pasteboard decoy hoax called `"THE BALLYHOO BIRD"`.
* **24 April 1879 (*The Daily Banner*, Brenham, TX, p. 1, col. 4, `CERT-1800-019`):** Reporting railroad debates, *The Bonham News* `"gives the colonel 'ballahoo.'"`—attesting Sense 1 (*give ballyhoo*) in unreduced nautical spelling.
* **4 June 1884 (*The Bellefonte Republican*, PA, p. 1, col. 3, `CERT-1800-021`) and 18 July 1884 (*St. Louis Post-Dispatch*, p. 5, col. 4, `CERT-1800-210`):** Tariff commentary predicts `"Democratic papers will raise another ballahoo"`, while baseball notes remark that no man `"can umpire Cleveland without getting ballahoo from the press"`—inserting maritime *ballahoo* into Anglo-Irish *raise / get Ballyhooly* syntax (`CERT-1800-207`) for loud public clamor.

## 4. Emergence and Diffusion on the American Midway (1893–1911)

Amusement trade weeklies show that *ballyhoo* entered outdoor showmanship in **July 1893** across three sequential stages:

### Stage A: The Free Outdoor Platform Spectacle and Hardware (1893–1899)

On **1 July 1893**, during the World's Columbian Exposition in Chicago, [*The New York Clipper* (Vol. 41, No. 17, p. 274, col. 2)](https://archive.org/details/per_new-york-clipper_new-york-clipper_1893-07-01_41_17/page/n13/mode/1up) (`CERT-1800-103`) ran an advertisement antedating *OED3*'s 19 November 1895 *Atlanta Journal* baseline by **2 years, 4 months, and 18 days** (and *HDAS* and *Green's* August 1901 baselines by **8 years and 1 month**):

> GOOD SIDESHOW TALKERS AND Ballyhoo Freaks Wanted. State lowest wages with board; twenty weeks' engagement at the World's Fair. Address International Amusement Company, Chicago, Ill.

Contrasting `"SIDESHOW TALKERS"` with `"Ballyhoo Freaks"` proves that *ballyhoo* first denoted the free outdoor crowd-gathering exhibition rather than the talker's speech. Mid-1890s notices confirm this platform sense: `"MUSICAL NOVELTY for Ballyhoo purposes"` in [*The New York Clipper* (28 September 1895, p. 478)](https://archive.org/details/per_new-york-clipper_new-york-clipper_1895-09-28_43_30/page/n13/mode/1up) (`CERT-1800-104`); Atlanta Exposition `"ballyhoos"` paired with `"coochee-coochee"` dancers in [*The Hustler of Rome* (25 November 1895, p. 4)](https://gahistoricnewspapers.galileo.usg.edu/lccn/sn89054007/1895-11-25/ed-1/seq-4/) (`CERT-1800-105`); `"SPECIALTIES ... for 'Bally Hoo' Stage, outside the Fair Ground Show"` with bugler or cornetist on [26 September 1896 (p. 478)](https://archive.org/details/per_new-york-clipper_new-york-clipper_1896-09-26_44_30/page/n11/mode/1up) (`CERT-1800-106`); a medicine-wagon `"ballyhoo gong"` on [19 March 1898 (p. 52)](https://archive.org/details/per_new-york-clipper_new-york-clipper_1898-03-19_46_3/page/n15/mode/1up) (`CERT-1800-107`); `"Fine Ballyhoo Umbrellas"` on [23 April 1898 (p. 134)](https://archive.org/details/per_new-york-clipper_new-york-clipper_1898-04-23_46_8/page/n15/mode/1up) (`CERT-1800-108`); and `"in front of the ballahoo stage"` in [*The New York Clipper* (30 December 1899, p. 925)](https://archive.org/details/per_new-york-clipper_new-york-clipper_1899-12-30_47_44/page/n12/mode/1up) (`CERT-1800-109`), preserving maritime *-lla-* on a carnival lot.

### Stage B: Metonymy to the Tent Barker, Denominal Verb, and Clipped *Bally* (1898–1910)

By autumn 1898, contiguity metonymy shifted *ballyhoo* from the platform spectacle to the outside pitchman. The [*St. Louis Post-Dispatch* (6 October 1898, p. 10)](https://archive.org/details/per_st-louis-post-dispatch_1898-10-06_50_50/page/n9/mode/1up) (`CERT-1800-110`) observes that `"Ballyhoo" is the latest vernacular for the gentleman who stands outside the tent` and formerly went by `"Barker"`. Nine weeks later, [*The New York Clipper* (10 December 1898, p. 700)](https://archive.org/details/per_new-york-clipper_new-york-clipper_1898-12-10_46_41/page/n13/mode/1up) (`CERT-1800-111`) sought a Norfolk store-show `"BALLYHOO MAN"` (15 years before *Etymonline*'s 1913 date), followed by circus and Buffalo Midway usage in [*The Billboard* (2 March 1901, p. 5)](https://archive.org/details/sim_billboard_1901-03-02_13_9/page/n4/mode/1up) (`CERT-1800-115`), [*The Scranton Tribune* (28 June 1901, p. 4)](https://panewsarchive.psu.edu/lccn/sn84026355/1901-06-28/ed-1/seq-4/) (`CERT-1800-112`), and Hartt's August 1901 benchmark (`CERT-1800-113`).

*The Billboard* immediately generated verbal and back-clipped derivatives: `"brought her out to 'bally-hoo' the crowd at Savannah"` on [8 December 1900 (p. 10)](https://archive.org/details/sim_billboard_1900-12-08_12_32/page/n9/mode/1up) (`CERT-1800-114`, **10 years and 1 month** before *OED3*'s 1911 verb entry); brass bands `"ballyhoo-ing" the streets` on [20 April 1901 (p. 12)](https://archive.org/details/sim_billboard_1901-04-20_13_16/page/n11/mode/1up) (`CERT-1800-116`, 1 year before *OED3* 1902); Adam `"bally-hooed" his little show` on [28 September 1901 (p. 11)](https://archive.org/details/sim_billboard_1901-09-28_13_39/page/n10/mode/1up) (`CERT-1800-117`); `"handle the announcement on the bally"` on [15 April 1905 (p. 20)](https://archive.org/details/sim_billboard_1905-04-15_17_15/page/n20/mode/1up) (`CERT-1800-212`); `"good man to bally on front"` on [18 August 1906 (p. 46)](https://archive.org/details/sim_billboard_1906-08-18_18_33/page/n45/mode/1up) (`CERT-1800-119`, **35 years and 8 months** before *Green's* 1942 verb baseline); `"collapsible bally stage"` on [25 May 1907 (p. 24)](https://archive.org/details/sim_billboard_1907-05-25_19_21/page/n23/mode/1up) (`CERT-1800-120`); and `"any good act for bally"` on [30 April 1910 (p. 25)](https://archive.org/details/sim_billboard_1910-04-30_22_18/page/n24/mode/1up) (`CERT-1800-122`).

### Stage C: Commercial, Political, and Motion-Picture Publicity (1902–1911)

Detached from tent fronts, *ballyhoo* broadened to excursion promotion (`"to ballyho the population into a trip to Omaha"`, [*The Billboard*, 3 May 1902, p. 8](https://archive.org/details/sim_billboard_1902-05-03_14_18/page/n7/mode/1up), `CERT-1800-118`), presidential campaigning in [*The Saturday Evening Post* (17 October 1908, p. 31)](https://archive.org/details/sim_saturday-evening-post_1908-10-17_181_16/page/n32/mode/1up) (`CERT-1800-121`), and film hype in [*The Nickelodeon* (18 February 1911, p. 195)](https://archive.org/details/nickelodeon05elec/page/n221/mode/1up) (`CERT-1800-123`: `"loudly ballyhooed releases"`, 8 years before *OED3* 1919).

## 5. Evaluating Competing Origins and Rejected Archival Leads

1. **County Cork *Ballyhooly* (1837) and Robert Martin's Song `"Ballyhooly"` (1886):** County Cork *Ballyhooly* cannot have fathered nautical *ballahoo*, as 1796 *Schr. Ballahoo* (`CERT-1800-007`) and 1810 *ballahou built schooner* (`CERT-1800-010`) predate August 1837 *ballyhooly* (`CERT-1800-205`) by 41 and 27 years. For terrestrial *ballyhoo, n.²*, however, Anglo-Irish *give / raise Ballyhooly* (`CERT-1800-207`) acted as a syntactic and phonological converger (`CERT-1800-016`, `CERT-1800-021`). Similarly, Robert Jasper Martin's December 1886 Gaiety song `"The Ballyhooley Blue Ribbon Army"`—tracked through [*The New York Mirror* (12 March 1887, p. 3)](https://archive.org/details/per_new-york-dramatic-mirror_the-new-york-mirror_1887-03-12_17_428/page/n2/mode/1up) (`CERT-1800-201`), [*Punch* (13 August 1887, p. 63)](https://archive.org/details/sim_punch_1887-08-13_93_2405/page/n3/mode/1up) (`CERT-1800-202`), Lew Dockstader's Broadway minstrel revival in [*The New York Clipper* (14 April 1888, p. 76)](https://archive.org/details/per_new-york-clipper_new-york-clipper_1888-04-14_36_5/page/n12/mode/1up) (`CERT-1800-203`), and Manus O'Conor's [*Irish Com-All-Ye's* (1901, p. 73)](https://archive.org/details/IrishCome-all-yes/page/n80/mode/1up) (`CERT-1800-204`)—likely reinforced noisy stage associations, but postdates 1877 `"THE GRAND BALLYHOO"` (`CERT-1800-208`) and 1879–1884 *ballahoo* (`CERT-1800-019`, `CERT-1800-210`).
2. **British Minced Oath *bally* (1884–1889):** Barrère and Leland's [*Dictionary of Slang, Jargon & Cant* (Vol. 1, 1889, p. 69)](https://archive.org/details/adictionaryofsla01barriala/page/n98/mode/1up) (`CERT-1800-211`) confirms that British *bally* (coined 1884–1885 in *The Sporting Times* for *bloody*) was strictly an adjectival/adverbial intensifier; US carnival *bally* (`CERT-1800-212`, `CERT-1800-119`) arose after 1901 by back-clipping *ballyhoo*.
3. **1893 Midway Arabic *bi-llāhi* / *B'Allah hoo* Folklore:** Floated in [*The New York Clipper* (19 October 1912, p. 6)](https://archive.org/details/per_new-york-clipper_new-york-clipper_1912-10-19_60_36/page/n3/mode/1up) (`CERT-1800-124`) and revived by Charles Wolverton and W. O. Taylor in [*The Billboard* (25 January 1936, p. 51)](https://archive.org/details/sim_billboard_1936-01-25_48_4/page/n51/mode/1up) (`CERT-1800-215`), the claim that 1893 Cairo or Turkish dervishes shouted `"B'Allah hoo"` fails a 5,914,177-character sweep across 15 contemporaneous 1893–1894 Midway volumes, including Bancroft's [*Book of the Fair* (Vol. 5, 1893, p. 864)](https://archive.org/details/bookfair5banca/page/n67/mode/1up) (`CERT-1800-213`) and Burnham's [*Sweet Clover* (1894, p. 274)](https://archive.org/details/sweetcloverroman00burn/page/n279/mode/1up) (`CERT-1800-214`), which record pidgin `"Alla gooda bum-bum"` and zero tokens of *ballyhoo* or *billahi*.
4. **Excluded Misdates, OCR Ghosts, and Homographs:** Scan verification eliminates bound-serial misdates stamping `1888` onto **8 January 1916** [*Collier's* (Vol. 56, p. 64)](https://archive.org/details/colliersnational56unse/page/n620/mode/1up) (`CERT-1800-125`), `1898` onto a **1932** Waukegan yearbook (`CERT-1800-216`), and `1897` onto **March 1936** [*The Survey* (p. 75)](https://archive.org/details/surveycharityorg72survrich/page/n74/mode/1up) (`CERT-1800-217`); OCR ghosts turning `"battalion of Rifles"` into `"ballahoo ot Killer"` in [*The New Era* (7 March 1856)](https://archive.org/details/NewEra_617/page/n1/mode/1up) (`CERT-1800-219`) and `"babyhood"` into `"ballyhoo'"` in [*The Columbia Spy* (23 March 1861)](https://panewsarchive.psu.edu/lccn/sn83032185/1861-03-23/ed-1/seq-3/) (`CERT-1800-017`); turf reports on the racehorse *Ballyhoo* (`CERT-1800-220`, `CERT-1800-102`); and Unalaska's *Mount Ballyhoo*, named by USRC *Bear* sailors after Melville's maritime phrase ([*Yachting*, October 1927, p. 52](https://archive.org/details/sim_yachting_1927-10_42_4/page/n22/mode/1up), `CERT-1800-221`).
