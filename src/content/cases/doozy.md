## What the sources establish

The best early evidence for standalone *doozy* remains Al Kleberg’s 1903 adjective. The 1901 rhyme is a real piece of evidence about early wordplay, but not enough to shift either of the standard adjective and noun dates.

### The 1901 rhyme

On June 1, 1901, *The Sunny South* published Oscar Ameringer’s “Little Bob and the Phonograph.” A phonograph plays adults courting in exaggerated baby talk. The paper prints “Oos woozy / doozy ducky is oo?” (the slash marks a line break; there is no printed hyphen). Nearby endearments are written in the same register. This may echo existing slang or simply be a rhyme coined for the scene; neither interpretation is established (CERT-3000-101).

### The established 1903 adjective

Kleberg’s story describes a succession of elaborate betting schemes. On [page 83](https://babel.hathitrust.org/cgi/pt?id=umn.31951001632845h&seq=87), the line reads “he began to evolve Schemes—one Doozy scheme followed the other.” The [copyright leaf](https://babel.hathitrust.org/cgi/pt?id=umn.31951001632845h&seq=6) dates the Phoenix Publishing Company copy to 1903. Dave Wilton previously identified this witness; the source check corrects the verb “involve” in his online transcription, not the date or his identification. The Baltimore imprint does not tell us where the word arose (CERT-3000-102).

### Nebraska word lists, 1911–1916

Louise Pound’s 1911 word list prints *doozy* as an adjective, “Sporty,” or “flossy” (CERT-3000-107). Elsie Warnock’s follow-up includes attributive and predicative examples, such as “She’s a trifle bit doozy.” Her original Part I is dated **1913**, even though the collected volume is dated 1917. Because Warnock points to Pound’s entry, the matching gloss is not independent evidence of two discoveries. The additional sentences support the usage without dating the speakers’ original utterances (CERT-3000-108).

Pound’s 1916 list prints *dozy, adj.* but gives “Isn’t that fish a dozy?” The article and position make the quoted token a count noun. The entry identifies an eastern Ohio contributor; it does not prove an origin in Ohio (CERT-3000-103).

## Origin: what remains open

[Daisy’s older approving uses](https://www.wordorigins.org/big-list-entries/doozy), including the examples from 1877 and 1883 reported by Wilton, give a credible semantic parallel but do not document the change to *doozy* (CERT-3000-104, CERT-3000-105). The Eleonora Duse story has no contemporary link established here. Neither the reported Carl Akeley letter (CERT-3000-106) nor a yacht name can advance the noun date without evidence of ordinary use and a secure date.

The [Indianapolis Motor Speedway Museum](https://www.indyautomuseum.org/explore/makes/duesenberg) places the Duesenberg Eight’s public debut in 1920. That model cannot account for a word printed in 1903. This does not exclude later association between car and slang (CERT-3000-109).
