## 1. Two Classical Mysteries and the Lexicographical Baseline

Few words in English historical lexicography pose a sharper double puzzle than *skulduggery* ('underhanded dealing, roguish political intrigue, chicanery'). As Michael Quinion observed in [*World Wide Words*](https://www.worldwidewords.org/ww-sku1.html) and Anatoly Liberman noted in his Oxford etymological commentary, connecting nineteenth-century American *skulduggery* to older Lowland Scots *sculdudrie* / *sculduddery* requires solving two seemingly abrupt transformations at once:

1. **The Semantic Leap (Kirk-Session Fornication to Legislative Caucus Fraud):** In eighteenth- and early nineteenth-century Scotland, [*sculduddery, n.*](https://www.oed.com/dictionary/sculduddery_n) (tracked from **1 September 1663** in the [*Dictionary of the Older Scottish Tongue*](https://dsl.ac.uk/entry/dost/sculdudry) and **1714** in the [*Scottish National Dictionary*](https://dsl.ac.uk/entry/snd/sculduddery)) meant 'fornication, adultery, unchastity' prosecuted before Presbyterian kirk sessions, or 'bawdry, obscene talk'. Yet when *scullduggery* and *skulduggery* emerge in mid-nineteenth-century American newspapers, they denote exclusively 'chaotic legislative maneuvering, back-room caucus trickery, ballot fraud, and peculation'—with zero sexual connotation.
2. **The Consonant Shift (`-dd-` to `-gg-` and `sc-` to `sk-`):** Why did the medial dental stop of Scots *sculduddery* (`/skʌlˈdʌd.ər.i/`) shift in North America to the velar stop of *scullduggery* and *skulduggery* (`/skʌlˈdʌɡ.ər.i/`), and why did nineteenth-century writers intermittently segment the word as *scull-duggery* or *skull-duggery*?

Major historical dictionaries long left a wide chronological gap over the American transition. In *OED1* (1914, Vol. VIII, s.v. *Sculduddery* sense 3), James Murray's team tentatively glossed the U.S. sense `"? Malversation of public money"` from Canon Isaac Taylor's **20 September 1890** query in *Notes and Queries*. The 1933 and 1986 *OED Supplements*—still unrevised in [*OED Online* (`skulduggery, n.`)](https://www.oed.com/dictionary/skulduggery_n) as modified in December 2025—pushed the entry back only to **1867**, quoting Albert Deane Richardson's [*Beyond the Mississippi*](https://archive.org/details/beyondmississipp00rich/page/134/mode/1up) (ch. XI, p. 134, CERT-2400-129): `"From Minnesota had been imported the mysterious term 'scull-duggery,' used to signify political or other trickery."` Mitford M. Mathews's *Dictionary of Americanisms on Historical Principles* (1951) and Barnhart's *Chambers Dictionary of Etymology* (1988) likewise opened at 1867; William A. Craigie and James R. Hulbert's *Dictionary of American English* (1944) reached **1863** (`"skullduggery"`, traced below to its primary weekly source in CERT-2400-125); *DARE* (Vol. IV, 2002) omitted a regional entry as general U.S.; and [*Green's Dictionary of Slang*](https://greensdictofslang.com/entry/m433yry) carried no headword entry at all. Meanwhile, the widely cited **1856** baseline in [*Etymonline*](https://www.etymonline.com/word/skulduggery) originated in Merriam-Webster's *Webster's Ninth New Collegiate Dictionary* (1983) slip file (calibrated alongside a **13 June 1856** Brookville *Indiana American* dispatch on the Sack of Lawrence in CERT-2400-114).

Crucial archival groundwork by [*Merriam-Webster* (28 August 2020)](https://www.merriam-webster.com/wordplay/the-words-of-the-week-august-28-2020), Michael Quinion (28 August 1858 *Burlington Daily Hawk-Eye*, CERT-2400-117), and independent researcher Bonnie Taylor-Blake in three landmark [*ADS-L* dispatches on 25 July 2021](https://listserv.linguistlist.org/pipermail/ads-l/2021-July/160120.html) (`160120`, `160121`, `160122`) transformed the antebellum timeline. Taylor-Blake pushed velarized `"scullduggery"` back to **9 December 1845** in Nashville's *The Politician* (p. 553) and **15 December 1845** in *The Republican Banner* (p. 1, CERT-2400-104), modern-spelled `"skulduggery"` to **30 January 1846** in *The Republican Banner* (p. 3, attributing the phrase to `"the gentleman from Tipton"`, CERT-2400-105), Iowa `"skullduggery"` to **18 August 1852** in the *Muscatine Journal* (p. 2, CERT-2400-106), St. Paul `"skullduggery"` to **25 May 1854** in *The Daily Minnesotan* (p. 2, CERT-2400-107), agentive `"skullduggers"` to **12 June 1855** (CERT-2400-113), and verbal `"skulldug"` to **16 September 1858** in Wyandotte, Kansas (CERT-2400-117, 78 years ahead of [*OED*'s 1936 William Faulkner baseline for `skuldug, v.`](https://www.oed.com/dictionary/skuldug_v)).

Building directly on that scholarship with full attribution, Case 2400 evaluates **66 primary-source, prosopographical, phonological, and red-team records** (59 accompanied by pre-1931 composite masthead and physical-ink scans). Together, they resolve both philological puzzles from primary evidence.

## 2. The Kirk-Session to Caucus Semantic Hinge (1663–1847)

Examining the Scottish archival record in full context reveals why *sculdudry* / *sculduddery* could shed its sexual restriction so cleanly: from its earliest appearances, the word was never a clinical anatomical term, but an **institutional satire on covert offences ferreted out by informers and settled behind closed doors**.

In its earliest surviving witness—a **1 September 1663** letter from Inveraray by Archibald Campbell, 9th Earl of Argyll, to Lord Clerk Register Sir Archibald Primrose ([Bannatyne Club, 1829, p. 34 fn.](https://archive.org/details/lettersfromarchi00bann/page/34/mode/1up), CERT-2400-101)—Argyll already uses `sculdudry` jocularly for a venial family scrape in contrast to felony theft (`"not for sculdudry, the old fault of the house, else I should not interpose, he would make a shift for friends; but for a less gentlemanny crime, theft"`). Exhaustive control sweeps of published sixteenth- and seventeenth-century ecclesiastical minutes—including *The Records of Elgin* (1544–1800), *Strathbogie* (1631–1654), and *Aberdeen*, alongside 1576 Orkney `Sculding` theft-slander inquests ([Maitland Club, 1859, p. 51](https://archive.org/details/oppressionsofsix00mait/page/51/mode/1up), CERT-2400-305)—yield zero occurrences of *sculdudry* in official clerk prose. Clerks wrote *fornication* or *harlotrie*; *sculdudry* was popular street slang mocking the pecuniary surveillance apparatus of the Kirk-Treasurer.

That institutional frame dominates eighteenth-century print:

- **Susanna Centlivre's *The Wonder: A Woman Keeps a Secret* (London, 1714, Act III, p. 43, [ECCO scan](https://archive.org/details/bim_eighteenth-century_the-wonder-a-woman-keep_centlivre-susanna_1714/page/43/mode/1up), CERT-2400-102):** Scottish footman Gibby invokes the exact enforcement triad—`"Ah, for a ruling Elder—or the Kirks Treaſurer—or his Mon... there wou'd na be ſo muckle Sculdudrie"`—glossed `"† Fornication"` at the foot of the page in a hit comedy performed continuously across British and American theaters into the 1840s.
- ***Anti-Priapeia* (1720, p. 15, [ECCO scan](https://archive.org/details/bim_eighteenth-century_anti-priapeia-or-an-an_ille-ego-qui-quondam_1720_0/page/n16/mode/1up), CERT-2400-304) and Allan Ramsay's *Poems* (Edinburgh, 1721 [composed 1714], p. 22, [ECCO scan](https://archive.org/details/bim_eighteenth-century_poems-by-allan-ramsay_ramsay-allan_1721_1/page/n53/mode/1up), CERT-2400-203):** [English Language & Usage contributor user499756](https://english.stackexchange.com/a/632421) previously quoted the p. 15 passage on 22 July 2025. *Anti-Priapeia* pushes the quadrisyllabic `-duddery` spelling (`"Muſt others pay for your Sculduddery, / As once it happen'd at Bahuddery?"`) back **101 years earlier** than Etymonline's 1821 date, while Ramsay's *"Elegy on John Cowper Kirk-Treasurer's Man, Anno 1714"* (`"Cou'd sa'r Sculdudry out like John"`) explains in its footnote how the Kirk-Treasurer's informer smelled out secret culprits so `"his Lordship makes pay to some purpose"` in hush-money fines.
- **Capt. Edward Burt's *Letters from a Gentleman in the North of Scotland* (London, 1754 [written c. 1730], Vol. I, p. 231, [ECCO scan](https://archive.org/details/bim_eighteenth-century_letters-from-a-gentleman_burt-edward-author-of-_1754_1/page/n252/mode/1up), CERT-2400-204):** Frames `"Sculduddery"` as a formal presbytery interrogation.

Between **1791 and 1835**, primary British and American imprints capture the exact four-step bleaching of the sexual feature and crystallization of the modern orthography **before** the word entered Tennessee legislative slang:

1. **1791 (`SKULL-DUDDERY` = Petty Covert Offences Below the Law):** In [*Prospects and Observations; on a Tour in England and Scotland*](https://archive.org/details/bim_eighteenth-century_prospects-and-observatio_thomson-william-lld_1791/page/n275/mode/1up) (London, 1791, p. 220, CERT-2400-306), Perthshire-born, St. Andrews-trained writer Dr. William Thomson (writing as `"Thomas Newte, Esq."`) describes parish elders `"watching, and reporting ſuch petty offences as are below the cognizance of the law, and which paſs under the general name of SKULL-DUDDERY."` Unrecorded in *OED*, *DOST*, and *SND*, this single 1791 passage antedates Sir Walter Scott's 1818 `sk-` spelling by **27 years**, antedates 1854 American `skull-` folk-etymology by **63 years**, and generalizes the meaning to petty sub-legal misconduct.
2. **1808–1825 (Ludicrous Tone and Complete Non-Sexual Bleaching in West-Country Scots):** John Jamieson's [*Etymological Dictionary of the Scottish Language*](https://archive.org/details/etymologicaldict02jami/page/n372/mode/1up) (Vol. II, 1808, CERT-2400-205) notes that `SCULDUDRY` was `"now used in a ludicrous manner"`, and his 1825 *Supplement* records west-country Scots `sculduddery` meaning simply `'rubbish, tatters; loose disorderly things'`.
3. **1818–1824 (Transatlantic Reprints, Parliamentary De-Eroticization, and Southwest Scots `-ll-`):** Sir Walter Scott's [*The Heart of Mid-Lothian*](https://archive.org/details/scotttalesofmylandlord2ndser02/page/n87/mode/1up) (Edinburgh, 1818, Vol. II, p. 78, CERT-2400-206)—reprinted in Philadelphia by D. Dickinson in **1821** ([p. 88](https://archive.org/details/talesmylandlord19scotgoog/page/n198/mode/1up), CERT-2400-210)—spread initial `sk-` (`"a wee-bit skulduddery for the benefit of the Kirk-treasurer"`) across American parlors, followed by Scott's [*Redgauntlet*](https://archive.org/details/redgauntlettale01scot/page/n256/mode/1up) in Edinburgh (**June 1824**, Vol. I, p. 249 `"speaking blasphemy and sculduddry"`, CERT-2400-209) and New York ([J. & J. Harper, 1824, Vol. II, p. 88](https://archive.org/details/redgauntletatal00scotgoog/page/n89/mode/1up) `"a little piece of sculduddery"`, CERT-2400-212). Even more pointedly, John Galt's [*The Ayrshire Legatees*](https://archive.org/details/ayrshirelegatee01galtgoog/page/n280/mode/1up) (serialized December 1820; Edinburgh book ed. **1821**, p. 271, CERT-2400-207; New York W. B. Gilley reprint **1823**, [p. 162](https://archive.org/details/ayrshirelegatee00galtgoog/page/n168/mode/1up), CERT-2400-211) has Presbyterian minister Dr. Zachariah Pringle comment on Queen Caroline's House of Lords trial by **explicitly setting aside the sexual charge** (`"putting, therefore, out of consideration the fact libelled... all the sculduddery of the business might have been well spared"`), transferring `sculduddery` directly onto unseemly parliamentary intrigue! Meanwhile, John Mactaggart's [*Scottish Gallovidian Encyclopedia*](https://archive.org/details/bub_gb_ivgvAAAAYAAJ/page/n434/mode/1up) (1824, p. 423, CERT-2400-208) proves that Galloway—the Southwest Scots departure hearth of Ulster-Scots Seceders—already spelled the word with geminate `-ll-` (`SCULLDUDDERY`) in Anti-Burgher Presbyterian preaching.
4. **1835–1847 (Naturalization in Philadelphia Periodicals and Emigrant Verse):** In **March 1835**, Philadelphia's [*The Lady's Book* (*Godey's*)](https://archive.org/details/sim_godeys-magazine_1835-03_10_3/page/128/mode/1up) (Vol. X, No. 3, p. 128, CERT-2400-103) printed `"scandal and sculduddery"` and `"provincial sculduddery"` twice in a sketch of clubroom nods, winks, and covert newspaper gossip ten years before the Nashville legislature, while Scots-American poet James M. Morrison still used `"Sculduddery"` on Philadelphia presses in [*Clarsach Albin*](https://archive.org/details/clarsachalbinoth00morr/page/n92/mode/1up) (**1847**, p. 81, CERT-2400-214).

## 3. From Tipton County, Tennessee, up the Mississippi Corridor to Lincoln's Desk (1845–1867)

Prosopographical reconstruction of the earliest American `-gg-` users reveals a single, continuous **Ulster-Scots Upland-Southern to Upper-Mississippi political and printing network**.

When `"scullduggery"` (`"great confusion"` on **9 December 1845**; procedural trickery on **15 December 1845**, CERT-2400-104) and canonical `"skulduggery"` (**30 January 1846**, CERT-2400-105) broke into print during the 26th Tennessee General Assembly in Nashville, Whig Representative John Watkins Richardson explicitly credited `"politically 'skulduggery,' (to borrow an expression of the gentleman from Tipton)"`. Charles A. Miller's [*Official and Political Manual of the State of Tennessee*](https://archive.org/details/bub_gb_0r0ZAAAAYAAJ/page/n229/mode/1up) (1890, pp. 216–217, CERT-2400-217) identifies `"the gentleman from Tipton"` as Whig Representative **Philip B. Glenn**, representing Tipton and Lauderdale Counties on the Mississippi River bluffs just north of Memphis—centered on Covington's heavily Ulster-Scots Associate Reformed and Old School Presbyterian settlement—while W. W. Clayton's [*History of Davidson County, Tennessee*](https://archive.org/details/historyofdavidso00clay/page/n339/mode/1up) (1880, pp. 235, 238, CERT-2400-218) establishes that Allen A. Hall edited *The Politician* and Whig Congressman **Washington Barrow** (succeeding Scots-named **Donald McLeod** in March 1845) edited *The Republican Banner*.

From West Tennessee's Mississippi landing, steamboat commerce and partisan newspaper exchanges carried the term directly upriver:

- **Muscatine, Iowa (August 1852):** As documented in [*The History of Muscatine County, Iowa*](https://archive.org/details/cu31924028914285/page/n481/mode/1up) (1879, p. 494, CERT-2400-219), the *Muscatine Journal* was revived from St. Louis and purchased in July 1852—four weeks before its **18 August 1852** `"general system of skullduggery"` editorial (CERT-2400-106)—by Indiana/Missouri Whig printers **Jacob and John Mahin**, who partnered the next year with Tennessee-born, St. Louis-trained printer **Orion Clemens**. Eleven years later, John Mahin's [*Muscatine Weekly Journal*](https://www.loc.gov/resource/sn84027253/1863-09-04/ed-1/?sp=1) (**4 September 1863**, p. 1, CERT-2400-127) was still denouncing `"political stock gamblers and skulduggers."`
- **St. Paul and Stillwater, Minnesota Territory (1854–1857):** After Ohio-born Whig editor John P. Owens charged gubernatorial `"collusion and 'skullduggery'"` in St. Paul's *Daily Minnesotan* on **25 May 1854** (CERT-2400-107), New York-born editor Thomas McLean Newson replied in [*The Minnesota Weekly Times*](https://www.loc.gov/resource/sn85025594/1854-05-30/ed-1/?sp=3) on **30 May 1854** (daily sub-header **26 May 1854**, Vol. I, No. 2, p. 3, col. 1, CERT-2400-108—our **12-year, 7-month open-scan antedating** of *OED* 1867):

> Judge Sherburne accordingly sentenced the prisoner on Monday, the 22d inst. to one year's imprisonment in the Penitentiary, and the Governor signed the document which gave him his freedom. Now in all this we impute no bad motives to Mr. Rosser—no “collusion or skulduggery”—neither do we think that Judge Sherbourne or even Gov. Gorman were actuated by any but the kindliest feelings towards the prisoner

Newson's own memoir [*Pen Pictures of St. Paul*](https://archive.org/details/penpicturesofstp01news/page/n399/mode/1up) (1886, p. 384, CERT-2400-223) confirms that every official in that dispute was an Anglo-American territorial appointee—Virginia-born Secretary Joseph Travis Rosser, Kentucky-born Democratic Governor Willis A. Gorman, and Maine-born Justice Moses Sherburne. Seven days later (**6 June 1854**, [p. 2, col. 4](https://www.loc.gov/resource/sn85025594/1854-06-06/ed-1/?sp=2), CERT-2400-109), Newson's shop quoted its own sentence as `"no collusion or skullduggery"`, demonstrating free `l`/`ll` variation. On **3 October 1854** (datelined St. Paul's, 25 September), the [*New-York Daily Times*](https://archive.org/details/sim_new-york-times_1854-10-03_4_949/page/n1/mode/1up) (p. 2, col. 4, CERT-2400-110, rescued from illegible Tesseract OCR) broadcast `"Skullduggery"` / `"skull-duggery"` nationally as St. Paul's signature political idiom. Stillwater's [*Saint Croix Union*](https://www.loc.gov/resource/sn83016847/1855-02-06/ed-1/?sp=2) followed with caucus `"fraud and 'skulduggery'"` on **6 February 1855** (p. 2, CERT-2400-111) and hyphenated `"skull-duggery"` under Presbyterian trustee Milton H. Abbott on **2 June 1855** ([p. 2](https://www.loc.gov/resource/sn83016847/1855-06-02/ed-1/?sp=2), CERT-2400-112), while Newson's [*Minnesota Weekly Times*](https://www.loc.gov/resource/sn85025594/1855-06-12/ed-1/?sp=1) minted agentive plural **`skullduggers`** (`"old, trickey, skullduggers"`, **12 June 1855**, p. 1, CERT-2400-113—still unrecorded in *OED* and *Merriam-Webster*). By **27 July 1857**, Republican Constitutional Convention President St. Andre Durand Balcombe could equate Bleeding Kansas fraud with `"this kind of skullduggery, as it is called in Minnesota"` ([*Debates and Proceedings*, 1858, p. 126](https://archive.org/details/cu31924032659298/page/126/mode/1up), CERT-2400-115).

From Minnesota and Iowa, the Lecompton Constitution crisis propelled the word across Kansas, Nebraska, Illinois, Washington, New York, and the Pacific Coast:

- **15 April 1858 (*The National Era*, Washington, D.C., [Vol. XII, No. 589, p. 58](https://archive.org/details/sim_national-era_1858-04-08_12_588/page/58/mode/1up), CERT-2400-116):** Quips that Kansas Surveyor-General John Calhoun should have attended `"some political school in Minnesota"` for `"a more thorough education in 'skull duggery'"` (properly dated via its printed 15 April running header despite a microfilm splice inside the 8 April reel).
- **7 December 1858 (*Meigs County Telegraph*, Pomeroy, Ohio, [p. 4, col. 1](https://www.loc.gov/resource/sn85038183/1858-12-07/ed-1/?sp=4), CERT-2400-118):** Prints the **phonological missing link**: `"'Scull-duddery' is the elegant term in use in Kansas to signify political trickery"`—preserving the unshifted Scots `-duddery` dental consonantism inside the exact Kansas political sense alongside Wyandotte verbal `"skulldug"` (CERT-2400-117).
- **1859 Frontier Pamphlet, Gold Rush Press, and the Medill–Lincoln Holograph:** C. Chaucer Goss's [*Bellevue, Larimer & Saint Mary*](https://archive.org/details/bellevuelarimers00goss/page/7/mode/1up) (Bellevue, Nebraska Territory, **1859**, p. 7, CERT-2400-119) provides the earliest standalone book/pamphlet attestation (`"with a skull-duggery that knows no tire"`), while [*The New-York Times*](https://archive.org/details/sim_new-york-times_the-new-york-times_1859-08-26_8_2475/page/2/mode/1up) (**26 August 1859**, p. 2, datelined Denver, 12 August, CERT-2400-120) carries `"miserable 'skulduggery'"` into Pike's Peak Gold Rush postal extortion. Two weeks later, on **10 September 1859**, *Chicago Press & Tribune* editor **Joseph Medill** penned a confidential postscript to **Abraham Lincoln** ([Library of Congress *Abraham Lincoln Papers*, `mal0187700`](https://www.loc.gov/item/mal0187700/), docketed `"J. Medill / Sep 10/59"` in Lincoln's hand, CERT-2400-121) regarding the Illinois Canal Scrip scandal and State Treasurer William Butler:

> You tell Butler that there must be no more skulduggery with the public moneys. J M

Completely unrecorded in prior etymological scholarship, Medill's holograph anticipates *OED1*'s 1914 `'Malversation of public money'` sense by **31 years**, followed in Medill's own [*Chicago Tribune*](https://archive.org/details/per_chicago-daily-tribune_1861-10-12_15_83/page/n1/mode/1up) on **12 October 1861** (p. 2, CERT-2400-122) by `"Oh Times! Oh sculduggery."`

- **1862–1867 National Generalization:** On **21 May 1862**, Columbus, Ohio's [*The Crisis*](https://archive.org/details/crisis02colu/page/133/mode/1up) (Vol. II, No. 17, p. 133, CERT-2400-123) denounced `"official sculduggery, (to use a Western phrase)"`—written by **Samuel Medary**, who had personally served as Territorial Governor of Minnesota (1857–1858) and Territorial Governor of Kansas (1858–1860). By **10 July 1862**, Portland's [*Morning Oregonian*](https://archive.org/details/morning-oregonian-1862-07-10/page/n2/mode/1up) (p. 3, CERT-2400-124) used `"skullduggery"` in a Multnomah County probate-court dispute. On **18 April 1863**, [*Frank Leslie's Illustrated Newspaper*](https://archive.org/details/sim_leslies-weekly_1863-04-18_16_394/page/51/mode/1up) (p. 51, CERT-2400-125) published the exact Missouri Unionist glossary item (`"two words to express the idea of underhanded plotting—to wit, 'skullduggery' and 'chenanigan'"`) later excerpted by Bartlett (1877) and *DAE* (1944), followed by New York comic weekly [*Vanity Fair*](https://archive.org/details/sim_vanity-fair-1859_1863-07-04_7_169/page/150/mode/1up) (**4 July 1863**, p. 150, CERT-2400-126) and non-political private divorce chicanery in [*The Louisville Daily Democrat*](https://archive.org/details/xt718911r137/page/n0/mode/1up) (**22 December 1865**, p. 1, CERT-2400-128), culminating in Richardson's **1867** memoir (CERT-2400-129).

## 4. Phonological Mechanism (`-dd-` to `-gg-`) and Deeper Scots Origins

Why did Scots `sculduddery` / `skulduddery` (`/skʌlˈdʌd.ər.i/`) become American `scullduggery` / `skulduggery` (`/skʌlˈdʌɡ.ər.i/`) while still coexisting with `Scull-duddery` in 1858 Kansas (CERT-2400-118)?

Primary phonological and lexical evidence converges on **progressive distant velar assimilation coupled with coronal dissimilation and Jacksonian `-uggery` / `-oggery` suffixal analogy**:

1. **Coronal Dissimilation and Velar Harmony:** Spoken rapidly, `/skʌlˈdʌd.ər.i/` stacks three successive coronal consonants (`/l ... d ... d/`) after an initial velar stop (`/k/`). Dissimilating the stressed-foot onset/coda from dental `/d/` to velar `/ɡ/` (`/k ... l ... d ... ɡ/`) breaks the coronal sequence by echoing the initial velar stop.
2. **Triply Reinforced Jacksonian Political Rhyme Template:** In 1830s–1845 American partisan invective—precisely where `scullduggery` debuted—three pejorative nouns in `/-ʌɡ.ər.i/ ~ /-ɒɡ.ər.i/` dominated legislative rhetoric: **`Humbuggery`** paired with `Wiggery` (*Columbia Democrat*, 19 August 1837, p. 2; *American Volunteer*, 16 April 1840, p. 3, [PA Newspaper Archive](https://panewsarchive.psu.edu/lccn/sn85025180/1837-08-19/ed-1/seq-2/), CERT-2400-308); **`Doggery`** ('low frontier grog-shop and upstairs legislative caucus den', analyzed by Leo Spitzer in *American Speech* 19 [1944]: 18–27 and verified in Anne Royall's [*Letters from Alabama*, 1830, p. 146](https://archive.org/details/lettersfromalaba00roya/page/146/mode/1up), the 1837 Athens *Southern Banner* `"a caucus held up stairs in the Doggery"`, and the 1839 *Huntingdon Journal*, CERT-2400-309); and **`hugger-muggery` / `hugger-mugger`** ('clandestine cabal, secret underhanded dealing', [Boyer 1797](https://archive.org/details/boyersroyaldict00boyegoog/page/n669/mode/1up) and the 4 February 1845 Macon *Georgia Telegraph* `"a hugger-mugger executive act"`, CERT-2400-310). Remodeling rare immigrant *scullduddery* onto the familiar *-uggery* political template of *humbuggery*, *doggery*, and *hugger-muggery* made an opaque Scots presbytery term immediately expressive to Jacksonian listeners, while secondary attraction to *skull* yielded hyphenated *skull-duggery* (1854–1865) just as it had earlier yielded Thomson's 1791 *SKULL-DUDDERY* (CERT-2400-306).

As for the deeper origin of Older Scots *sculdudry* (1663, CERT-2400-101), lexical audits across historical dictionaries clarify the competing scholarly proposals:

- **Internal Scots Pejorative *duddery* + *skuld-* / Expressive Prefixation (Strongest Evaluated Pathway):** Jamieson's (1808, CERT-2400-315) comparison with Continental German/Dutch *schuld* ('fault, guilt') fails initial `/sk-/` if borrowed from Dutch (`/sx/`), whereas **Old Norse *skuld*** ('debt, due, fault, fine', verified in Cleasby & Vigfusson's [*Icelandic-English Dictionary*, 1874, p. 562](https://archive.org/details/bub_gb_RnEJAAAAQAAJ/page/n679/mode/1up), CERT-2400-318, and 1576 Orkney `Sculding`, CERT-2400-305) preserves velar `/sk-/` in Scots and combines cleanly with Scots pejorative *duddery* / *dudrie* ('raggedness, tatters, low disorder', from *duds* 'rags', accounting for Kincardineshire 'rubbish, tatters' in Jamieson's 1825 *Supplement* and *SND*).
- **Weakened Romance, Gaelic, and Scandinavian Proposals:** Deriving *sculdudry* from Old French *escolorgier* ('to slip', [Godefroy, Tome III, 1884, p. 414](https://archive.org/details/GodefroyDictionnaire3/page/n425/mode/1up), CERT-2400-316) lacks any attested noun `*escoulourgerie` and leaves `/r.dʒ/ -> /d.r/` unexplained. Irish glossary `Sgaldruth` ('fornicator', copied from Lhuyd 1707 and O'Brien 1768 into [William Shaw's *Galic and English Dictionary*, 1780](https://archive.org/details/agalicandenglis00shawgoog/page/n323/mode/1up), CERT-2400-317) is omitted from both the 1828 *Dictionarium Scoto-Celticum* and Macbain (1911, p. 311) as an English-loan compound (`scald + drúth`, silent `-th`). Finally, Prof. Barbara P. Harris's (1991) Upper-Midwest Scandinavian *skyldig + -eri* hypothesis (calibrated against 1890 Swedish [`skyldig`](https://archive.org/details/newpocketdiction00leipuoft/page/90/mode/1up) in CERT-2400-319)—formulated when *OED*'s 1867 entry pointed to Minnesota—is outweighed by five independent findings: 1845–1846 Tennessee attestations predating Minnesota Territory (1849), Upland-Southern/Anglo-American actor prosopography (CERT-2400-217, CERT-2400-223), coexisting 1858 Kansas `Scull-duddery` (CERT-2400-118), unattestation of `*skyldigeri`, and Swedish/Norwegian alveolo-palatal `/ɧ ~ ʃ/` onset phonology.

## 5. Falsified Folk Myths and Archival Red-Team Audit (Traps A–D)

Direct page-scan inspection eliminates three popular etymological myths and ten archival false positives that routinely corrupt automated searches:

- **No Pre-1860 Underworld Cant `*duggery` ('Cheating'):** Complete alphabetical sweeps of Francis Grose (1785), [*Lexicon Balatronicum* (1811, leaf n81)](https://archive.org/details/b29337781/page/n81/mode/1up), and George W. Matsell's *Vocabulum* (1859 copyright; c. 1877 R. Worthington reprint) confirm `DUDDER` and `DUDS`, with **zero entries** for `*duggery` or `skulduggery` (CERT-2400-313). The modern web claim of cant `*duggery = 'cheating'` is a garbled conflation of John S. Farmer and W. E. Henley's [*Slang and Its Analogues*](https://archive.org/details/slangitsanalogue02farmuoft/page/302/mode/1up) (Vol. II, 1891, p. 302, CERT-2400-314) `"DOGGERY, subs. (popular).—1. Transparent cheating"` (from Carlyle, post-1858), while Joseph Wright's *English Dialect Dictionary* (1900–1904) confirms zero `-gg-` forms in British regional dialect.
- **No Grave-Robbing ("Skull-Digging") or Pirate Origin:** Negative controls across Edinburgh's Burke-and-Hare trial book [*West Port Murders* (1829, p. 118)](https://archive.org/details/westportmurderso00burkrich/page/118/mode/1up) and Capt. Charles Johnson's *General History of the Pyrates* (1724) yield zero occurrences of `skulduggery` or `skull-digging`; body-snatchers were called `resurrectionists` (CERT-2400-320).
- **Trap B Dirty-OCR Ghosts (`BUGGERY` and `doggery` Misread as `duggery`):** Apparent eighteenth-century English legal hits for `"DUGGERY"` in Richard Burn's [*Justice of the Peace* (1756, Vol. I, p. 161)](https://archive.org/details/bim_eighteenth-century_the-justice-of-the-peace_burn-richard_1756_1/page/n183/mode/1up) are worn capital-`B` OCR misreadings of `"1. BUGGERY"` (CERT-2400-323), while tempting antebellum Southern newspaper hits for `"go up to the duggery and liquor"` in the [*Fayetteville Observer* (9 August 1843, p. 1)](https://archive.org/details/per_fayetteville-observer_fayetteville-observer_1843-08-09_27_1366/page/n0/mode/1up) and *Spirit of the Times* (29 May 1847) print faint-arch lowercase `o` in **`doggery`** (CERT-2400-324).
- **Trap A Serial Misdates and Trap C/D Intrusions:** Visual title-page and masthead checks eliminate bound-serial founding-year mirages in [*The Yale Literary Magazine*](https://archive.org/details/yaleliterarymag06unkngoog/page/n458/mode/1up) (cataloged `1836`, printed **June 1871**, p. 445, CERT-2400-002), [*Iowa Legislative Documents*](https://archive.org/details/legislativedocu58assegoog/page/n817/mode/1up) (cataloged `1866` Pennsylvania, printed **1880** Des Moines, p. 578, CERT-2400-131), James S. Buck's [*Milwaukee Under the Charter*](https://archive.org/details/pioneerhistorym04buckgoog/page/n76/mode/1up) (cataloged `1850`, printed **1886**, paired with 1887 *Try-Square* cataloged `1807`, CERT-2400-321), and [*Notes and Queries*](https://archive.org/details/s7notesqueries10londuoft/page/224/mode/1up) (cataloged `1849`, printed **20 September 1890**, p. 224, paired with 1893 *Medical and Surgical Reporter* `"druggery"` cataloged `1858`, CERT-2400-322), alongside Continental Germanic legal `skuldug` ('indebted', [*Diplomatarium Norvegicum*, 1847](https://archive.org/details/diplomatariumno00bugggoog/page/n413/mode/1up), CERT-2400-132), County Tyrone townland `Duggery` (`Doogary` in [Earl of Belmore, 1881](https://archive.org/details/historytwoulste00belmgoog/page/n42/mode/1up)), and mis-cataloged twentieth-century books and editorial glossaries (CERT-2400-325).
