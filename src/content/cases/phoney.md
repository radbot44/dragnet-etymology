## Executive Summary and Lexicographical Calibration

Few American colloquialisms hide as sharp a transatlantic hinge as ***phoney*** (more commonly spelled ***phony*** in the United States). In its March 2006 revision (modified March 2026), the [*Oxford English Dictionary*](https://www.oed.com/dictionary/phoney_adj) concludes that *phoney, adj. and n.* is "Probably a variant of *fawney* n."—the eighteenth-century Anglo-Irish thieves'-cant noun for a gilt-brass ring used in confidence swindles, borrowed from Irish [*fáinne*](https://dil.ie/21121) ('finger-ring'). Yet three empirical gaps have long kept that derivation hedged with "probably" and invited rival folk etymologies invoking early telephones or an imaginary brass-jewelry manufacturer named "Forney":

1. **The Pre-1893 Chronological Chasm and the 1862 Civil War Outlier:** *OED3* brackets an isolated 25 April 1862 Confederate letter (`"a phoney scrape and also a serious one, too"`, CERT-2200-105), opens its adjectival record at 29 June 1893 in the *Chicago Daily Tribune* (`"'phony' bookmakers"`, CERT-2200-110), and dates the noun (`B.`) only from 1902 (plural *phonies* at 28 November 1916). Building on [Peter Tamony's 1937 *American Speech* study](https://muse.jhu.edu/article/456460/summary) and [Paul S. Cohen's 2011 phonological analysis](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1467-968x.2011.01247.x), [Dave Wilton (*Wordorigins.org*, 10 February 2022)](https://www.wordorigins.org/big-list-entries/phoney) pushed print attestations back to a 4 April 1890 *Detroit Plaindealer* telephone-marriage pun (`"quite a 'phoney' joke"`, CERT-2200-108) and an unambiguous 7 May 1892 Washington *Evening Star* baseball report (`"a little on the phony order"`, CERT-2200-109).
2. **The Missing Occupational Bridge (`fawney man` -> `phoney man`):** [*Green's Dictionary of Slang*](https://greensdictofslang.com/entry/4stjsfa) dates American *fawny men* ('peddlers of bogus jewelry') to 1899 and *phony men* to 30 June 1900 (*National Police Gazette*, CERT-2200-216)—leaving a seven-year gap after 1893 racetrack *phony* during which skeptics could claim betting-ring *phony* preceded jewelry-pitchman *phoney*.
3. **Unverified `<fawney>` -> `<foney>` -> `<phoney>` Spellings and Edition Traps:** Digitized nineteenth-century corpora teem with optical-character mirages and misdated reprints—most notoriously Cornell University's undated scan of George W. Matsell's *Vocabulum; or, The Rogue's Lexicon* (CERT-2200-314), widely cited as `1859` despite having had its title-page date chiseled off the stereotype plate.

Case 2200 audits 72 primary witnesses (70 pre-1931 composite proof scans) across Irish lexicography, Old Bailey trials, cant glossaries, jewelry trade journals, turf sheets, and penny dailies to close all three gaps:

- **Direct 1894 Occupational Continuity:** In February 1894, Josiah Flynt published an illustrated essay in *The Century Magazine* defining American tramp `"fawny men"—fellows who sell bogus jewelry` made of $2-a-dozen Buffalo gilt brass (CERT-2200-206; syndicated in the *St. Louis Post-Dispatch* on 6 March 1894, CERT-2200-107), antedating *Green's* 1899 baseline by 5 years, 7 months. Six months later, on 15 August 1894, the Maysville *Evening Bulletin* reported from nearby Hamilton, Ohio: `"Harry Murphy, a 'Phoney' jewelry man, was arrested on Hight street yesterday in the act of selling a gold watch for $3"` (CERT-2200-114)—antedating *Green's* June 1900 *phony men* baseline by **5 years, 10 months**, followed by `"phony" stones` in *The Jewelers' Circular* on 2 January 1895 (CERT-2200-115).
- **Five `<f->` Bridges and Pre-1902 Nominal Antedatings:** Un-remodeled `<f->` spellings survive across 1889–1914 (`fauny` / `fauney` in 1889, CERT-2200-205; `foney coin` alongside `"phony" delegations` in the 7 March 1897 *St. Louis Post-Dispatch*, CERT-2200-209; `foney watch deal` in 1899, CERT-2200-214; `fauny shop` in 1903, CERT-2200-220; and `selling foney jewelry` in a January 1905 Joplin police circular, CERT-2200-218). Nominal *phoney, n.* is pushed back **6 years, 8 months** ahead of *OED3*'s 1902 baseline across racetrack tickets (`"his pasteboards were all 'phonies'"`, 20 July 1895, CERT-2200-116—**21 years, 4 months** ahead of *OED3*'s 1916 plural; `"She was a phony"`, 15 December 1896, CERT-2200-118), counterfeit coin (`"a 'phony'"`, 9 April 1896, CERT-2200-208), and a pawned fake diamond stud (`"learned it was a 'phony'"`, 4 May 1897, CERT-2200-210).

## Phase I: Irish *fáinne* and the Anglo-Irish *Fawney* Chain (1768–1867)

Old and Middle Irish [*áinne*](https://dil.ie/1351) ('ring, circle') acquired prothetic *f-* through lenition reanalysis (*an fháinne* / *an áinne*), yielding Modern Irish [*fáinne*](https://dil.ie/21121) ('finger-ring'). John O'Brien's 1768 Paris *Focalóir Gaoidhilge-Sax-Bhéarla* (p. 228, CERT-2200-301) records `"FÁIN & Fàinne, a ring; rectius áin, áinne"`, and Edward O'Reilly's 1817 Dublin *Sanas Gaoidhilge-Sagsbhearla* labels the *f-* form vernacular (`"Áinne, s. f. vulg. fáinne, a ring"`, paired with Dinneen's 1904 `fáinne an phósta, wedding ring`, CERT-2200-302). Kuno Meyer and John Sampson's 1890–1891 studies of Irish Traveller Shelta (*Journal of the Gypsy Lore Society*, CERT-2200-303) prove that Shelta disguised *fáinne* as `gránya` via `f- -> gr-`; London thieves' cant therefore borrowed `fawney` directly from spoken Hiberno-Irish. In southern and Munster Irish, stressed *á* before nasals rounded strongly to `[ɔː]`—the same rule documented by P. W. Joyce (1910, p. 214, CERT-2200-304) in `bawn` / `bawneen`—mapping `[ˈfˠɔːnʲə]` onto English `fawney` `[ˈfɔːni]`.

In 1781, London actor George Parker described `"The FAWNEY RIG"` in *A View of Society and Manners in High and Low Life* (Vol. II, pp. 166–167, CERT-2200-305): an accomplice drops a `"graved, double-gilt, flat, brass ring"` before a passerby, claims half-shares, and extracts nine or ten shillings for gilt brass bought in a London shop `"for the purpose of going on the Fawney."` High-resolution inspection of Parker's page resolves a classic paleographic trap: on `"THE Fawney ſays"`, the italic capital `F` bears a distinct horizontal crossbar contrasting on the same line with uncrossbarred medial long-s `ſ` (`ſays`, `ſome`, `ſhop`, `ſold`), ruling out `Sawney`.

Sporting and flash reprints carried *fawney* into American print well before the Civil War: New York's *Spirit of the Times* printed `"Fawney your famms"` ('put rings on your hands') on 22 July 1843 (CERT-2200-102), followed by *The New York Clipper*'s `"34, Fawnies—Rings"` and `"faunied famms"` on 22 September 1860 (CERT-2200-104) and the Philadelphia *Daily Evening Telegraph*'s `"a ring a 'fawney'"` on 26 November 1867 (CERT-2200-106).

Collating the Library of Congress copyright-deposit title leaf (`"Filed May 23. 1859"`, printing intact `1859.` and first-state `SPECIAL JUCTICE`) and the University of Michigan 1859 first edition of George W. Matsell's *Vocabulum* (CERT-2200-313) against Cornell University's scan (`cu31924073798740`, CERT-2200-314) proves that Cornell's copy is an undated **c. 1877 R. Worthington stereotype reissue** with `1859.` chiseled off the title plate and an 1876–1877 rear book catalog. In both states, Matsell omitted `FAWNEY` under `F`, misprinted `PARNEY. A ring.` on p. 64, defined `ATTLEBOROUGH` on p. 9 as base-metal imitation jewelry, and adapted Ducange Anglicus's London flash dialogue on p. 105 (CERT-2200-312), correcting Ducange's misprinted `'sawney'` back to `'for the fawney'`.

## Phase II: Phonological Closing, `<ph->` Remodeling, and the 1894 Pitchman Hinge

Formal 1868–1886 New York detective books—anchored by Thomas Byrnes's *Professional Criminals of America* (1886, CERT-2200-203), which calls jewelry-tray ring-switchers `"pennyweight-workers"` with zero hits for *fawney*, *foney*, or *phoney*—show that the shift unfolded sub-literarily among itinerant street and fairground grifters. Meanwhile, *OED3*'s bracketed 25 April 1862 letter from Pvt. Benjamin Franklin Moody of the 35th Georgia Infantry (`"I will tell you of a phoney scrape and also a serious one, too"`, CERT-2200-105) places `phoney` in direct antonymic contrast with `serious` as a rural Georgia Piedmont phonetic spelling of ***funny*** ('amusing, non-lethal', parallel to `PHUNNY PHELLOW` and Josh Billings's `PHUNNY MAN`, CERT-2200-319), followed by a thirty-year print vacuum.

Two mechanisms converted cant `fawney` into American `phoney`:

1. **Pre-Nasal Vowel Raising and Closing (`[fɔːni]` -> `[foʊni]`, `<fauny>` / `<foney>`):** Before intervocalic `/n/`, rounded `[ɔː]` raised and closed to `[oʊ]` in North American underworld speech. Five primary witnesses preserve `<f->` spellings: Barrère and Leland's `"fashino fauny"` / `"fauney"` (1889, CERT-2200-205); `"take in foney coin"` on p. 26 of the same 7 March 1897 *St. Louis Post-Dispatch* issue that printed `"phony" delegations` on p. 6 (CERT-2200-209); *The Jewelers Review*'s `"foney watch deal"` (28 June 1899, CERT-2200-214); Hutchins Hapgood's `"fauny shop (imitation jewelry)"` (May 1903, CERT-2200-220); and Joplin Police Chief J. A. McManamy's January 1905 circular for yeggmen `"selling foney jewelry"` (CERT-2200-218), summarized in *Dialect Notes* (1914, `"Also foney, fony, phoney"`, CERT-2200-215).
2. **Neo-Greek `<ph->` Remodeling and the 1890 Telephone Pun:** By 1890, *telephone* (`phone`), *phonograph*, and *symphony* had normalized `phon-` for `/foʊn/`. On 4 April 1890, the *Detroit Plaindealer* (Vol. 7, No. 46, p. 2, CERT-2200-108) printed `QUITE "PHONEY."` above an item on two night telephone operators married in jest over the wires without a license (`"passed off as quite a 'phoney' joke"`), exploiting the homophony between spoken `/foʊni/` ('sham') and `phone + -y`. On 7 May 1892, the Washington *Evening Star* (p. 12, CERT-2200-109) used `"a little on the phony order"` for a fluke four-base hit without telephone wordplay, followed in summer 1893 by betting-ring reports in the *Chicago Daily Tribune* (29 June 1893, CERT-2200-110), *St. Louis Post-Dispatch* (6 July 1893, glossing `'phony'` as `"an element of fraud"`, CERT-2200-111), and *New York Times* (2 August 1893, CERT-2200-112).

The occupational hinge surfaces directly in 1894. Following J. P. Johnston's account of circus prize-soap wrapping in *Twenty Years of Hus'ling* (1889, CERT-2200-204), Josiah Flynt's February 1894 *Century Magazine* article (pp. 520, 522, CERT-2200-206) depicted `"A FAWNY MAN"`—peddlers hawking $2-a-dozen Buffalo brass rings as stolen gold. Six months later, Hamilton, Ohio police arrested Harry Murphy as `"a 'Phoney' jewelry man"` selling a $3 watch (15 August 1894 Maysville *Evening Bulletin*, CERT-2200-114), initiating an unbroken chain across *The Jewelers' Circular* (`"phony" stones`, 2 January 1895, CERT-2200-115; `"phony" rings`, 1 September 1897, CERT-2200-211), Eldridge and Watts (`"fawny" watch`, 1897, CERT-2200-119), *St. Louis Post-Dispatch* (`"'PHONY' MEN"` and `"phony coin"`, 18 November 1898, CERT-2200-212), *The Lima News* (`"selling phony jewelry"` plus 200 counterfeit nickels, 30 November 1898, CERT-2200-213), *National Police Gazette* (`"phony men, who peddle bogus jewelry"`, 30 June 1900, CERT-2200-216), *Fort Wayne Sentinel* (`"phoney" ring peddler`, 17 June 1902, CERT-2200-219), *Saturday Evening Post* (circus `"phoney men"`, 28 November 1903, CERT-2200-221), and *The Billboard* (`"foney wheel"` and `"tripes and kiester"` pitchmen, 29 November 1913, CERT-2200-223).

## Phase III: Pre-1902 Nominal Conversion and Generalization (1894–1907)

Reinforced by Henry M. Blossom Jr.'s 1896 Chicago turf novel *Checkers* (p. 224, CERT-2200-002), *phoney* converted into a noun (`phoney, n.`) nearly seven years before Clarence Louis Cullen's 1902 *More Ex-Tank Tales* (*OED3* baseline, mis-cited as *Six Ex-tank Tales*; cf. Cullen's 1900 `phony keys`, CERT-2200-121):

- **Betting Tickets and Racehorses (1895–1896):** On 20 July 1895, the *St. Louis Post-Dispatch* (p. 8, CERT-2200-116) reported `"his pasteboards were all 'phonies'"` (**6 years, 8 months** before *OED3* 1902; **21 years, 4 months** before its 1916 plural), followed by unquoted `"She was a phony"` in the *Daily Racing Form* on 15 December 1896 (CERT-2200-118).
- **Counterfeit Coin and Imitation Jewels (1896–1907):** On 9 April 1896, the *Daily Racing Form* (p. 1, CERT-2200-208) printed `"a 'phony'"` for a brass pool check passed as a $5 gold piece; on 4 May 1897, the *St. Louis Post-Dispatch* (p. 3, CERT-2200-210) printed `"learned it was a 'phony'"` for a pawned fake diamond stud (11 years before *Green's* 1908 jewel noun baseline), followed by *The Jewelers' Circular-Weekly* (`"a 'phony' substituted"`, 17 April 1901, CERT-2200-217) and uncount `"PASSING PHONY"` (8 November 1907, CERT-2200-222).
- **Political and Social Insincerity (1894–1902):** Adjectival *phoney* generalized to `"phony" delegation` (13 May 1894, CERT-2200-113), `"the 'phoney' Napoleon"` (26 July 1896, CERT-2200-117), and George Ade's `"Phoney Acting"` in *More Fables* (1900, CERT-2200-003), where an English visitor cannot comprehend the Americanism—matching Farmer and Henley's 1902 London P-volume (CERT-2200-004), which steps from `PHOENIX-NEST` to `PHYSIC` with zero entry for *phoney*.

## Phase IV: Falsified Etymologies and Archival Traps (Traps A–D)

1. **Telephone and Wire-Tapper Conjectures:** *The Century Dictionary Supplement* (1909, p. 994, CERT-2200-224) suggested `[phone + -y1]`, while popular lore credited poolroom wire-tappers. Yet *The National Police Gazette* (21 January 1905, CERT-2200-316) and Arthur Train (1908) prove that fake wire-tappers tapped **Western Union telegraph lines** after `"Selling phony money [green goods] grew to be a pretty poor calling"`, importing preexisting *phony* from counterfeit-money grifters.
2. **The `"Forney Jewelers"` Eponym Myth:** Auditing all 76 Attleboro-district jewelry manufacturers in the 1878 *Massachusetts Register* and 9.44 million characters of *The Jewelers' Circular* (1875–1892, CERT-2200-317), alongside *The Keystone*'s October 1888 stage-jewelry report and contemporary Forney biographies (CERT-2200-318), confirms **zero `"Forney"` jewelry firms existed**. Instead, Pierce Egan (1830 `"diamond forney"`, CERT-2200-309) and Farmer and Henley (1893 `"FORNEY, subs. (thieves').—A ring; a variant of FAWNEY"`, CERT-2200-001) show that non-rhotic cant `forney` (`= fawney`) was retroactively reinterpreted as a manufacturer's surname.
3. **Trap A–D Scan Falsifications:** Physical ink inspection eliminates line-wrap `"tele- / phone,"` (8 December 1883 *Decatur Daily Republican*, CERT-2200-321) and `"Sym- / phony"` (1890–1891 *Musical Year-Book* and Philip Hale's 1930 scrapbook misdated `1889`, CERT-2200-125); broken-type `"money"` -> `phoney` (1838, CERT-2200-322; 11 June 1883, CERT-2200-123), `"forty men"` -> `fony men` (28 September 1879, CERT-2200-226), `"David Roby"` -> `David phoney` (1875, CERT-2200-322), and `"Fancy"` -> `Foney` (1857, CERT-2200-322); proper-name and joke-column homographs (`Foney` for Clerk John W. Forney in 1850 and Abbott's `"Phonny"`, CERT-2200-320; `"the Phony man"` = `"the Funny man"` in 24 January 1885 *Chicago Daily Tribune*, CERT-2200-225); and serial/edition misdates on the April 1910 *Coast Review* (`1871`, CERT-2200-323), November 1927 *Writer's Monthly* (`1912`, CERT-2200-227), and 1962 *Roget's Thesaurus* (`1852`, CERT-2200-126).
