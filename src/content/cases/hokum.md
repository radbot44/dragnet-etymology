## 1. Prior Scholarship and Lexicographical Baseline

Unrevised since the 1933 *Supplement*, the [*Oxford English Dictionary*](https://www.oed.com/dictionary/hokum_n) dates *hokum, n.* to **1917** (*New York Sun*) and hedges its origin as *"perhaps formed within English, by blending: Etymons: hocus-pocus, bunkum"*—echoed by [*Merriam-Webster*](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/hokum) and [*Etymonline*](https://www.etymonline.com/word/hokum). Unrevised 1976 *Supplement* entries date [*hoke, v.*](https://www.oed.com/dictionary/hoke_v) to **14 February 1935** and [*hokey, adj.*](https://www.oed.com/dictionary/hokey_adj) to **1945** (*New York Times*), omitting *hoke, n.* and *hokum, adj.*.

Recent historical-slang scholarship has narrowed several of those gaps:

* **Early Newspaper and Fiction Benchmarks (*hokum, n.* and *adj.*):** [*Green's Dictionary of Slang*](https://greensdictofslang.com/entry/sq7l3na) and Dave Wilton ([*Wordorigins.org*, 1 May 2026](https://www.wordorigins.org/big-list-entries/hokum)) cited Roy L. McCardell's chorus-girl monologue in the [*New York Evening World* of **3 March 1906** (p. 9, cols. 2–4)](https://www.loc.gov/resource/sn83030193/1906-03-03/ed-1/?sp=9) (`CERT-1600-102`, where the scan prints *"write one myself"*, correcting a secondary typo *"myelf"*), Kenneth McGaffey's [*The Sorrows of a Show Girl* (September 1908, p. 214)](https://archive.org/details/sorrowsofshowgir00mcga/page/214/mode/1up) (`CERT-1600-103`, Sense 2: *"spring that sure-fire hokum"*), and Philadelphia's [*Evening Public Ledger* of **13 February 1915** (p. 4, col. 4)](https://archive.org/details/per_evening-public-ledger_evening-public-ledger_1915-02-13_1/page/n3/mode/1up) (`CERT-1600-104`, [*Green's* baseline for *hokum, adj.*](https://greensdictofslang.com/entry/glsy4dy): *"Al Jolson's 'Hokum' Stuff"*). On the American Dialect Society mailing list ([ADS-L 169645, 19 July 2026](https://listserv.linguistlist.org/pipermail/ads-l/2026-July/169645.html)), Fred Shapiro pushed McCardell's usage back six weeks to an italic sub-headline in the [*Evening World* of **20 January 1906** (seq. 9)](https://www.loc.gov/resource/sn83030193/1906-01-20/ed-1/?sp=9) (`CERT-1600-101`: *"Everything in It Is Sure Fire Hokum"*, misread in OCR as *"Hofum"*).
* **Derivative Benchmarks (*hoke, v.* and *hokey, adj.*):** [*Green's*](https://greensdictofslang.com/entry/h3r7xoy) dates *hoke up* to **14 February 1925** and simplex *hoke, v.* to **1928**, and with Wilton ([*Wordorigins.org*, 2 September 2026](https://www.wordorigins.org/big-list-entries/hokey)) cites [*Variety* of **29 June 1927** (p. 31, col. 1)](https://archive.org/details/sim_variety_1927-06-29_87_11/page/n30/mode/1up) (`CERT-1600-220`: *"good hokey act"*) for [*hokey, adj.*](https://greensdictofslang.com/entry/5z5r6qa). On **15 October 2026** ([ADS-L 170180](https://listserv.linguistlist.org/pipermail/ads-l/2026-October/170180.html)), Shapiro antedated *hokey, adj.* to [*Variety* of **19 May 1922** (p. 10, col. 2)](https://archive.org/details/variety66-1922-05/page/n89/mode/1up) (`CERT-1600-105`: *"It's just hokey frolic"*). Rob King's [*Hokum!*](https://www.ucpress.edu/books/hokum/paper) (2017) traced the word's shift from low vaudeville to cinema.
* **Refuting the December 1856 *Punch* Lookalike:** Replying to Shapiro on **19 July 2026** ([ADS-L 169647](https://listserv.linguistlist.org/pipermail/ads-l/2026-July/169647.html)), Bill Mullins flagged two December 1856 tokens of *hokum* in *Punch*. Scans of [**6 December 1856** (Vol. 31, p. 222)](https://archive.org/details/punch30a31lemouoft/page/n505/mode/1up) (`CERT-1600-118`, `CERT-1600-318`) and [**20 December 1856** (p. 248)](https://archive.org/details/punchvol30a31lemouoft/page/600/mode/1up) (`CERT-1600-309`) show that convict "James Hardy" pairs Cockney *h*-dropping (*ands*, *ard*, *onest*) with hypercorrect *h*-prothesis (*"'arf as hesy"*, *"Horstralia"*, *"turn a crank or work a mill, or tese hokum"* = tease oakum).

## 2. Primary-Source Chronology: From 1899 Booking Cards to *Variety*'s "Oakum" Controversy

Informal accounts often assume that *hokum* originated among nineteenth-century circus barkers or medicine-show pitchmen. Yet full-text sweeps across eighteen digitized circus memoirs, minstrel gag books, and patent-medicine exposes from 1863 to 1911—totaling **20,860,294 characters**, including W. C. Coup's [*Sawdust & Spangles* (1901)](https://archive.org/details/sawdustspangless00coup/page/136/mode/1up) (`CERT-1600-201`), Frank Dumont's [*Witmark Amateur Minstrel Guide* (1 May 1899)](https://archive.org/details/witmarkamateurmi00dumo/page/n5/mode/1up) (`CERT-1600-202`), and Samuel Hopkins Adams's [*The Great American Fraud* (1906)](https://archive.org/details/greatamericanfra00adamuoft/page/n7/mode/1up) (`CERT-1600-203`)—plus Roy L. McCardell's [*The Show Girl and Her Friends* (1904, 109,242 characters)](https://archive.org/details/showgirlherfrien00mcca/page/n9/mode/1up) (`CERT-1600-122`) return **zero** tokens of *hokum*, *hocum*, *hoke*, or evaluative *hokey*. Outdoor pitchmen adopted *hokum* in *The Billboard*'s "Pipes for Pitchmen" only on [**23 August 1913** (p. 33, col. 2)](https://archive.org/details/sim_billboard_1913-08-23_25_34/page/n32/mode/1up) (`CERT-1600-208`).

Instead, *hokum* emerged in the **Philadelphia–Wilmington–New York variety and minstrel circuit** during the late 1890s. Following the farce character *"Silas Hokum, a new postmaster"* in [*The New York Clipper* (**28 September 1889**, leaf n9)](https://archive.org/details/per_new-york-clipper_new-york-clipper_1889-09-28_37_29/page/n9/mode/1up) (`CERT-1600-204`), the *Clipper* reported on [**14 October 1899** (Vol. 47, No. 33, leaf n10, col. 2)](https://archive.org/details/per_new-york-clipper_new-york-clipper_1899-10-14_47_33/page/n10/mode/1up) (`CERT-1600-205`) that *"The members of Hokum Club, of that city [Philadelphia], presented him [vaudevillian William Silbor] with a neatly engraved medal for his bravery."* Two weeks later, in [*The New York Clipper* of **28 October 1899** (Vol. 47, No. 35, p. 732, col. 4)](https://archive.org/details/per_new-york-clipper_new-york-clipper_1899-10-28_47_35/page/n19/mode/1up) (`CERT-1600-106`, `CERT-1600-206`), Irish sketch team Ed. Bryan and Madge Norman—quoting Wilmington, Delaware minstrel manager W. L. Dockstader—published our earliest verified lexical witness:

> BRYAN AND NORMAN MADE THE BIGGEST HIT EVER MADE BY AN IRISH SKETCH TEAM IN MY HOUSE.—W. L. DOCKSTADER, WILMINGTON, DEL., OCT. 21.
> ED. BRYAN and MADGE NORMAN PRESENTING CASEY'S ST. PATRICK'S DAY ORATION.
> TO MANAGERS.—DON'T book this act if your audience likes hokum or gravey acts, or if you have a hickey audience that is looking for slate [sic, for slap] stick stuff; neither is this act suitable for a Dutch audience.

Lowercase, unglossed, and unquoted beside *gravey* ('easy broad laughs'), this card shows *hokum* routine booking jargon by autumn 1899.

Sweeping *Variety* from 16 December 1905 explains its early silence: while McCardell used *"Sure Fire Hokum"* in 1906 (`CERT-1600-101`) and Channing Pollock glossed *"what vaudeville people call 'sure fire hokum'"* in [*The Smart Set* (**November 1908**, p. 152)](https://archive.org/details/sim_smart-set_1908-11_26_3/page/152/mode/1up) (`CERT-1600-109`), Sime Silverman standardized `/ˈhoʊ.kəm/` in *Variety* as **`"oakum"`**—on [**26 October 1907** (p. 11, col. 2)](https://archive.org/details/sim_variety_1907-10-26_8_7/page/n10/mode/1up) (`CERT-1600-107`: *"rough humor, commonly known as 'oakum.'"*) and [**12 December 1908** (p. 5, col. 3)](https://archive.org/details/sim_variety_1908-12-12_13_1/page/n4/mode/1up) (`CERT-1600-108`: *"feeding oakum acts to oakum audiences"*). On [**8 May 1909** (*Variety*, Vol. 14, No. 9, p. 9, col. 2)](https://archive.org/details/sim_variety_1909-05-08_14_9/page/n8/mode/1up) (`CERT-1600-110`), comedian Bert Somers challenged *Variety*'s spelling, drawing Silverman's reply:

> I say the word is "hocum." He says it is "oakum" ... I have heard old timers for years back speak of stuff, and comedians as being "hocum," also "hoecake." ... *Bert Somers*, (of Somers and Wible).
> ["Oakum" is the correct term. ... The nearest approach to "hocum" (there is no word of that spelling) may have been suggested through "hocus" or "hocus-pocus," referring to sleight-of-hand or juggling. ...—Ed.]

This exchange confirms that oral *hocum* (and *hoecake*) circulated *"for years back"*, unmasks Walter J. Kingsley's Cockney *"hoakum"* myth in H. L. Mencken's [*The American Language* (1936, p. 189)](https://archive.org/details/in.ernet.dli.2015.169487/page/n211/mode/1up) (`CERT-1600-225`) as an echo of Silverman's 1907–1909 spelling (dropped via *"Hoakum and Gravy"* in July 1910 and *"hokum"* in August 1910, `CERT-1600-111`, after *The Billboard*'s `"put over the hokum"` on **7 May 1910**, `CERT-1600-207`), and proposes *hocus-pocus* twenty-four years before the 1933 *OED Supplement*.

## 3. Unidirectional Word-Family Derivation: *Hokum* (1899) to *Hoke* (1913–1918) and *Hokey* (1919)

Theatrical weeklies from 1880 to 1930 fix a strictly unidirectional derivational chain:

1. **Attributive *hokum, adj.* (1899 / 1910–1911):** Modifier usage opens with *"hokum or gravey acts"* (**28 October 1899**, `CERT-1600-106`), followed by *"pronounce a few hokum words over it"* in [*The New York Clipper* (**17 September 1910**, p. 765, col. 5)](https://archive.org/details/per_new-york-clipper_new-york-clipper_1910-09-17_58_31/page/n0/mode/1up) (`CERT-1600-112`, `CERT-1600-210`) and *"'hokum' stuff"* in [*Variety* (**28 January 1911**, p. 21, col. 1)](https://archive.org/details/sim_variety_1911-01-28_21_8/page/n20/mode/1up) (`CERT-1600-211`), four years ahead of *Green's* 1915 benchmark (`CERT-1600-104`), before spreading to Broadway, silent film (`CERT-1600-113`), and minstrel *"Hokum, Blues Harmony"* (`CERT-1600-209`).
2. **Back-Formed Noun *hoke, n.* (11 April 1913):** Parallel to *bunkum* → *bunk* (1900), speakers clipped `-um` to form nominal *hoke* in [*Variety* of **11 April 1913** (p. 18, col. 3)](https://archive.org/details/sim_variety_1913-04-11_30_6/page/n17/mode/1up) (`CERT-1600-114`, `CERT-1600-212`: *"It may be hoke, but it is great stuff"*), followed on [**15 October 1915** (p. 5, col. 2)](https://archive.org/details/sim_variety_1915-10-15_40_7/page/n4/mode/1up) (`CERT-1600-213`: *"Raz-ma-taz, hoke and jaz!"*) and [**12 May 1916** (p. 42)](https://archive.org/details/sim_variety_1916-05-12_42_11/page/n41/mode/1up) (`CERT-1600-214`: *"Pile Hoke upon Hoke and then add more Hokum"*).
3. **Zero-Derived Verb *hoke, v.* (25 January 1918) and Phrasal *hoke up* (9 May 1919):** Nominal *hoke* converted to transitive verbal use in [*Variety* of **25 January 1918** (p. 36, col. 2)](https://archive.org/details/sim_variety_1918-01-25_49_9/page/n35/mode/1up) (`CERT-1600-215`: *"Lew Hawkins just 'jazzed' and 'hoked' them out of their seats"*), seventeen years pre-*OED* (1935), and to *"hoking a good deal at a typewriter"* and *"couldn't seem to make himself understand [sic] so he hoked it up"* on [**9 May 1919** (pp. 10, 25)](https://archive.org/details/sim_variety_1919-05-09_54_11/page/n24/mode/1up) (`CERT-1600-115`, `CERT-1600-216`).
4. **Rhyme-Catalyzed *hokey, adj.* (25 July 1919 & 22 October 1920):** Evaluative *hokey* ('banal, corny') debuts in [*Variety* of **25 July 1919** (p. 33, col. 2)](https://archive.org/details/sim_variety_1919-07-25_55_9/page/n32/mode/1up) (`CERT-1600-116`, `CERT-1600-217`) via rhyme on *pokey*: *"trivial, convention [sic] and almost banal, ... mostly pokey and mainly 'hokey.'"* Unquoted *"a nice hokey routine"* follows on [**22 October 1920** (p. 9, col. 3)](https://archive.org/details/sim_variety_1920-10-22_60_9/page/n8/mode/1up) (`CERT-1600-218`) and *"very hokey"* on [**21 January 1921** (p. 37, col. 2)](https://archive.org/details/sim_variety_1921-01-14_61_8/page/n36/mode/1up) (`CERT-1600-219`), over twenty-five years pre-*OED* (1945).

## 4. Evaluating Origin Hypotheses and Archival Traps

### The *Hocus* × *Bunkum* Blend and Showfolk Contact Hypothesis

Blending four-syllable *hocus-pocus* with *bunkum* looks awkward only if one overlooks clipped **Hocus** (`/ˈhoʊ.kəs/`, 'cheat; conjurer's trickery'), attested since 1654 in Grose's [*Vulgar Tongue* (1785, sig. O2r)](https://archive.org/details/bim_eighteenth-century_a-classical-dictionary-o_grose-francis_1785/page/n109/mode/1up) (`CERT-1600-314`) and Farmer and Henley's [*Slang and Its Analogues* (1893, III:324)](https://archive.org/details/slangitsanalogue03farm/page/324/mode/1up) (`CERT-1600-305`, `CERT-1600-315`). Paired with American **bunkum** (`/ˈbʌŋ.kəm/`, attested **27 September 1828** in Thornton's [*American Glossary*, 1912, I:121](https://archive.org/details/americanglossary014322mbp/page/n138/mode/1up), `CERT-1600-315`, and Bartlett's [*Dictionary of Americanisms*, 1848, p. 54](https://archive.org/details/dictionaryofamer00bartuoft/page/54/mode/1up), `CERT-1600-306`), trochaic *hocus* and *bunkum* overlap on shared medial `/kə/`: `ho[kə]s` × `bʌŋ[kə]m` → `hokum` (`/ˈhoʊ.kəm/`).

Three pre-1899 traditions plausibly reinforced that blend:

* **British Folk-Drama Patter (1857–1888):** L. P. Barnaschone's [*Cambrian Journal* (1857, IV:194)](https://archive.org/details/cambrianjournal00instgoog/page/n203/mode/1up) (`CERT-1600-304`, `CERT-1600-316`; repr. Chambers's [*Book of Days*, 1864, II:741](https://archive.org/details/bookofdaysmiscel02cham/page/740/mode/1up), `CERT-1600-117`) records the Tenby Mummers' Quack-Doctor bottle *"Called hokum, skokum, alicompane"* (`< hocum-pocum`).
* **Mid-Atlantic Anglo-Romani Cant (1873–1882):** Charles Godfrey Leland ([*English Gipsies*, 1873, pp. 89, 149](https://archive.org/details/englishgipsiest00lelagoog/page/n108/mode/1up), `CERT-1600-301`, `CERT-1600-313`; [*The Gypsies*, 1882, p. 348](https://archive.org/details/gypsies00lela/page/348/mode/1up), `CERT-1600-303`) and George Borrow ([*Romano Lavo-Lil*, 1874, p. 51](https://archive.org/details/romanolavolilwor00borruoft/page/50/mode/1up), `CERT-1600-302`) attest *hokkeny* / *Hokkano* / *hokkani* ('trick, swindle') and *Hocben* / *hukaben* ('lie, humbug') among Philadelphia *"jugglers and 'show-people'"*.
* ***Hokey-Pokey* vs. Dialect Lookalikes:** Farmer and Henley (1893, III:330, `CERT-1600-307`) record *hokey-pokey* for 'cheat' and 'cheap street ware', catalyzing the 1919 *"pokey / 'hokey'"* rhyme, whereas Joseph Wright's [*English Dialect Dictionary* (1902, III:201, 255)](https://archive.org/details/englishdialectdi03wriguoft/page/201/mode/1up) (`CERT-1600-308`, `CERT-1600-317`) rules out regional *hoke* ('gore; dig') and Irish *by the hokey* (1789).

### Rejected Archival Leads

Nineteen rejected ledger records flag recurring pre-1920 false leads:

* **Catalog and Reprint Misdates:** Internet Archive `theatre1900newy` ([*Theatre Magazine*, indexed `"1900"`](https://archive.org/details/theatre1900newy/page/375/mode/1up), `CERT-1600-319`) is December **1922**; a Denison catalog for Harlan Tarbell's *Crazy Stunts* behind a 1906 copyright is **1929** (`CERT-1600-119`, `CERT-1600-222`); Wilfred Granville's *Dictionary of Theatrical Terms* ([indexed `"1912"`](https://archive.org/details/in.ernet.dli.2015.458764/page/n97/mode/1up), `CERT-1600-119`) is **1952**; and a [`"1912"` *UCLA Daily Bruin* reel](https://archive.org/details/ucladailybruin166losa/page/n289/mode/1up) (`CERT-1600-223`) is **23 February 1996**.
* **OCR Ghosts and Homographs:** Scans eliminate `"E. I. Horsman"` misread as `"E. f. Hokum an"` (*Harper's Weekly*, 16 November 1889, `CERT-1600-320`), `"Roster"` misread as `"Hokum-"` (*Clipper*, 23 February 1907, `CERT-1600-120`), `"looked"` / `"choking"` misread as `"hoked"` / `":hoking"` (`CERT-1600-224`), Benjamin D. Silliman's Babylon estate *"Hokum"* (*New York Times*, **8 December 1899**, `CERT-1600-321`), circus surname *Hocum* (`CERT-1600-121`, `CERT-1600-221`), Mayor *Hocum Hosford* (`CERT-1600-310`), Malay-Arabic *Khususol Hokum* (`CERT-1600-311`), and tramp stew *hokum-snivvy* (`< 1874 Hook um snivey`, `CERT-1600-312`).
