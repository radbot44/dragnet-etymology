## 1. Prior Scholarship and Lexicographical Baseline

In the Oxford English Dictionary (malarkey, n.) (CERT-2700-105), the June 2008 revised entry labels the word "of unknown origin" and cites International News Service sportswriter Davis J. Walsh's September 8, 1923 Firpo dispatch ('a lot of "malarkey," as it were') as earliest unambiguous semantic evidence, noting San Francisco cartoonist Thomas Aloysius "Tad" Dorgan's earlier comic-strip usage. Full priority credit belongs to the lexicographers who established the pre-1924 framework:

1. Dave Wilton (Wordorigins.org, Dec. 24, 2020, CERT-2700-105), Jonathon Green (Green's Dictionary of Slang, CERT-2700-308), Ben Zimmer (Vocabulary.com, 2012), and Mark Liberman (Language Log, 2016): Pinpointed Tad Dorgan's March 9, 1922 San Francisco Call and Post "Indoor Sports" panel alongside a November 25, 1894 Sydney Truth 'Captain Mullurky' military joke.
2. Peter Tamony (Collection C3939, CERT-2700-310), Michael Quinion (World Wide Words, 2006, CERT-2700-309), Anatoly Liberman (Oxford Etymologist, 2007), and William Sayers (Western Folklore 61, 2002): Evaluated the Irish surname Mullarkey/Malarkey, British dialectal marlock, and Mediterranean malakia hypotheses.

## 2. Calibrating Tad Dorgan (1922–1924), Davis J. Walsh (Sept. 1923), and Stage-Irish Precursors

### 2.1 "YES MILARKEY 609 J" (March 9, 1922) vs. Davis J. Walsh's Semantic Breakthrough (Sept. 8–10, 1923)

Primary facsimile and newspaper-run inspection resolves two persistent misconceptions about the 1922–1924 birth of malarkey:

* Tad Dorgan's March 9, 1922 Panel (CERT-2700-105) & April 2, 1924 "MALACHY" (CERT-2700-110): In the March 9, 1922 San Francisco Call and Post "Indoor Sports" panel (preempted in East Coast Hearst papers like the Boston American by local boxing cartoons), Dorgan's hand-lettered speech balloon reads YES MILARKEY 609 J.—a mock stage-Irish telephone-exchange gag (MI-larkey 609 J) rather than 'Malachy' or an unambiguous common noun for 'nonsense'. Two years later, Dorgan deployed comic exclamation MALACHY — YOU SAID IT in The Capital Times (April 2, 1924, p. 11) (CERT-2700-110), while exhaustive sweeps of Dorgan's Daffydils (1911), Indoor Sports (1914), and 10,987 issues of the Daily Racing Form yield zero pre-1922 slang tokens.
* Davis J. Walsh's September 8–10, 1923 INS Syndicate Breakthrough (CERT-2700-107, CERT-2700-108): Same-day wire scans in The Kingston Daily Freeman (Sept. 8, 1923, p. 12) (CERT-2700-107) preserve Walsh's Dempsey–Firpo training-camp dispatch ('a lot of "malarkey," as it were'), while two days later the South Bend News-Times (Sept. 10, 1923, p. 11) (CERT-2700-108) elevated the word into a bold subhead—Lot of "Malarkey."—antedating headline display by two years.
* Stage-Irish Character & Prosodic Precursors (1890–1924): Pre-1922 American comic print saturated Irish-American humor with stock Mullarkey/Malachy characters associated with tall talk and blarney: "MRS. MULLARKEY" in Puck (Feb. 19, 1890, p. 437) (CERT-2700-102), "Mrs. Mullarkey" / "Killarney" rhyme play in Kate Douglas Wiggin's Penelope's Irish Experiences (1901, p. 102) (CERT-2700-103), smooth-talking idler "young Malachy" in The Chicago Sunday Tribune (June 2, 1907, Pt. 5, p. 3) (CERT-2700-104), Chicago Irish comic verse pairing "Duffy, McGuffy, Mullarky" with "blarney an' banter" in Patrick G. Smyth's Rhyme with Reason (1911, pp. 165, 168) (CERT-2700-206), and the Irish-bull archetype "Mullarky" in Stewart Anderson's Sparks of Laughter (1924, p. 251) (CERT-2700-112).
