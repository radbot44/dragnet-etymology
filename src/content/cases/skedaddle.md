## 1. Prior Scholarship and Lexicographical Baseline

In William A. Craigie's 1911 First Edition fascicle of the Oxford English Dictionary (Vol. IX, Pt. I, bound 1919, p. 128, CERT-2600-308), skedaddle, v. was dated to 1862 and skedaddle, n. to 1870, while OED Online (CERT-2600-309) currently cites an August 10, 1861 New-York Daily Tribune report ("skiddaddled") for the verb, retains 1870 for the noun, and labels the origin obscure or uncertain despite 1860s claims of British dialectal 'spill milk' usage. Over the past five decades, specialist lexicographers steadily pushed the pre-Civil War frontier record earlier—and receive full priority credit here:


1. Richard Lyle Power (1942), Gerald Cohen (1979), Barry Popik, Dave Wilton & Green's Dictionary of Slang: Power (Indiana Magazine of History 38, 1942), Gerald Cohen (Comments on Etymology VIII/10–11, 1979), ADS-L researchers (Dec. 2004), Dave Wilton (Wordorigins.org, Nov. 10, 2021, CERT-2600-310), and Jonathon Green (Green's Dictionary of Slang, CERT-2600-309) pushed skedaddle, v. prior to Fort Sumter via the January 12, 1860 Wellsboro Agitator (PA) Missouri/Hoosier flatboat anecdote, while Anatoly Liberman (An Analytic Dictionary of English Etymology, 2008, pp. 186–189, CERT-2600-310) dismantled classical Greek, Swedish, and Gaelic pseudo-etymologies in favor of English dialectal scaddle + frequentative -addle.
2. Stephen Goranson & Mark Liberman (2025): On Language Log (July 10, 2025) (CERT-2600-311), Stephen Goranson and Mark Liberman pushed the same Missouri/Hoosier "Chickenville" flatboat story into 1859 via the August 13, 1859 Red Wing Sentinel (MN).
2. June 23, 1859 Antedating, the 1850->1859 Minimal-Pair Proof, and the Missouri Border-War Pivot
2.1 Pushing the Print Record to June 23, 1859 and Catching the Exact Substitution for scatter
Our antebellum newspaper sweep advances the earliest verified print witness of the skedaddle family to June 23, 1859—seven weeks earlier than the August 13, 1859 Red Wing Sentinel and 6.5 months earlier than the January 12, 1860 Wellsboro Agitator—and uncovers the verbatim 1850 ancestor of the famous Hoosier flatboat story:


* June 23, 1859 (The Kenosha Telegraph and Tribune, p. 1, col. 4, CERT-2600-101): In "A Pike's Peak Song", signed by M. Loraine Taylor of Geneva, Wisconsin, disillusioned Pike's Peak gold-seekers turn back at St. Joseph, Missouri on June 1, 1859:"The first day of June we got to St. Joe— / Our teams in fine order because we drove slow; / We washed ourselves up, and our hair we did comb, / Got aboard of the cars and skedaddled for home— / For it's hard times."
* The 1850 -> 1859 Minimal-Pair Substitution Proof (CERT-2600-102 to CERT-2600-104): Scanning the "Chickenville / Franklin" Missouri flatboat yarn across the August 13, 1859 Red Wing Sentinel ("ske- / daddle", CERT-2600-102), December 13, 1859 Daily Pittsburgh Post (p. 2) (CERT-2600-103), and January 12, 1860 Wellsboro Agitator (p. 1) (CERT-2600-104) enabled us to locate its verbatim 1850–1852 parent text in The Star of the North (April 25, 1850, p. 1) and Palmetto Standard (July 14, 1852, p. 4), where the identical closing sentence originally read "You'd oughter see the gang scatter." A summer-1859 newspaper exchange editor substituted newly fashionable Missouri-corridor "skedaddle" directly into "scatter"'s syntactic slot.
* Continuous Pre-Sumter & Pre-Bull Run Missouri–Kansas–Platte Corridor (Feb.–July 1861): Before First Bull Run (July 21, 1861), skedaddle flourished in Sol. Miller's White Cloud Kansas Chief (Feb. 28, 1861, p. 2) (CERT-2600-105), Rocky Mountain correspondence in Wilkes' Spirit of the Times (March 23, 1861, p. 36) (CERT-2600-106), and border-war reporting ten days before Manassas in the White Cloud Kansas Chief (July 11, 1861, p. 2) (CERT-2600-107).




2.2 Resolving OED's August 10, 1861 Baseline, skedaddle, n. (-8 yrs, 5 mos), and Upstate NY Fort Skadaddle
Physical ink inspection of OED's canonical earliest quotation—New-York Daily Tribune (Aug. 10, 1861, p. 5, col. 6) ("skiddaddled", CERT-2600-109)—proves it was not a Virginia/Bull Run coinage: the Tribune explicitly credits "From The Missouri Democrat", datelined BETHANY, Harrison Co., Mo., July 31, 1861, explaining the word as Northwest Missouri / Southern Iowa Union Home-Guard slang—corroborated by the Louisville Daily Journal (Feb. 3, 1862, p. 4) (CERT-2600-112) and Chicago Daily Tribune (March 12, 1862, p. 2) (CERT-2600-113), which testified to ten-year pre-war Iowa oral currency. Meanwhile, in Northern Virginia, Sgt. J. C. Otis's October 23, 1861 letter in The Rebellion Record, Vol. III (CERT-2600-111, paired with New-York Daily Tribune, Oct. 18, 1861, p. 5, CERT-2600-110) proves Capt. William N. Angle of the 35th NY Volunteer Infantry (Jefferson/Lewis Counties, Upstate New York) named abandoned Confederate earthworks "Fort Skadaddle."


Rapid morphological radiation followed immediately:


* Nominal skedaddle, n. & Participial skedaddling (July 25, 1861, 8 yrs, 5 mos earlier than OED 1870): "ANOTHER 'SKEDADDLE.'" and "'Skedaddling' expedition" in White Cloud Kansas Chief (July 25, 1861, p. 2) (CERT-2600-108), followed in national magazine prose by "Saddle vs. Skedaddle" and "Skedaddle horses" in Vanity Fair (Aug. 23, 1862, pp. 88, 92) (CERT-2600-203).
* Agentive skedaddler, n. (May 9, 1862) & Skedaddledom (June 18, 1862): "the skedaddlers driven South" in Louisville Daily Democrat (May 9, 1862, p. 1) (CERT-2600-114), "far down in 'Skedaddledom'" in Urbana Union (June 18, 1862, p. 3) (CERT-2600-115), and Pennsylvania's 1866 draft-dodger disfranchisement statute known as the "Skedaddler Act" in The Bedford Gazette (April 24, 1868, p. 2) (CERT-2600-218).
* Presidential, Musical, Literary & Colonial Adoption (1862–1892): John C. Molter's Chicago sheet music "Skedaddle" Comic Song (1862) (CERT-2600-213), Abraham Lincoln's July 19, 1863 autograph manuscript verse ("And we skedaddled back again") in the Brown University Digital Repository (bdr:72535) (CERT-2600-214), Frank Moore's Songs of the Soldiers (1864, p. 94) (CERT-2600-215), Anthony Trollope's The Last Chronicle of Barset (1867, Vol. I, p. 260) (CERT-2600-216), and New South Wales Minister James Inglis's Tent Life in Tigerland (1888/1892, p. 107) (CERT-2600-217).












3. The 1862 Transatlantic Controversy and Proof That Printed British 'Spill' Citations Postdate October 1862
Between May and December 1862, skedaddle sparked a major transatlantic philological controversy across The Historical Magazine (May–June 1862, pp. 163, 196) (CERT-2600-201), The Press (Aug. 2, 1862, p. 1) (CERT-2600-202), Rowland Clegg-Hill, 3rd Viscount Hill's famous October 13, 1862 London Times letter reprinted in Notes and Queries (Oct. 25, 1862, p. 326) (CERT-2600-204, asserting Dumfriesshire/Ayrshire milkmaids said "you are skedaddling all that milk"), the October 1862 London Spectator rebuttal in The Rebellion Record, Vol. VI (1863, p. 10) (CERT-2600-205) copied verbatim by John Camden Hotten's Slang Dictionary (1864, p. 232) (CERT-2600-207), Maximilian Schele De Vere's Americanisms (1872, pp. 284–285) (CERT-2600-208), and John Russell Bartlett's Dictionary of Americanisms, 4th Ed. (1877, pp. 596–598) (CERT-2600-209).


Crucially, our forensic audit of every British dialect glossary before and after 1862 proves that 100% of printed British 'spill milk/coals' citations postdate Lord Hill's October 1862 letter:


* Zero Pre-1862 British *skedaddle Entries: William Dickinson's Glossary of the Words and Phrases of Cumberland (1859, p. 102) (CERT-2600-210), directly across the Solway Firth from Dumfriesshire, contains zero entries for skedaddle (as do Jamieson 1808/1825, Brockett 1829/1846, Halliwell 1847, and Thomas Wright 1857).
* Every Citation in Joseph Wright's EDD Vol. V (1904, p. 458, CERT-2600-307) Dates to 1877–1894: Wright's four cited authorities all postdate Civil War fame by 15 to 32 years—including The Atlantic Monthly (Aug. 1877, p. 234) (CERT-2600-211) and Charles Mackay's Dictionary of Lowland Scotch (1888, p. 191) (CERT-2600-212, echoing Lord Hill's milk-pail example; cf. Scottish National Dictionary).
