# Case 3300 Origin and Early Word-Family Monograph: `bogus` (`TGT-004`)


## Unmasking the "c. 1798 *Band of Brothers*" Mirage

For three decades, modern discussions of *bogus* have been split between two incompatible starting dates. The *Oxford English Dictionary* (`bogus, n.1 & adj.`) and *Dictionary of Americanisms* open the record with Eber D. Howe's July 6 and November 2, 1827 *Painesville Telegraph* reports on a Geauga County, Ohio counterfeiting ring whose cast-iron coining apparatus was dubbed a *Bogus* (`CERT-3300-105`, `CERT-3300-106`, `CERT-3300-306`). In 1994, however, Volume I of J. E. Lighter's *Random House Historical Dictionary of American Slang*—subsequently summarized by Michael Quinion at *World Wide Words* (2000) and *Grammarphobia* (2014)—cited a work titled *Band of Brothers* dated **c. 1798**: `"Coney means Counterfeit paper money ... Bogus means spurious coin"`. If genuine, a 1798 cant glossary would invert the semantic history of *bogus*, making the counterfeit-coin sense twenty-nine years older than the 1827 Ohio coining press.

Primary-scan collation resolves this discrepancy as a classic **Trap-A internal-charter misdating** (`CERT-3300-201`). No 1798 imprint titled *Band of Brothers* exists in Evans's *American Bibliography*, Sabin, or OCLC. The verbatim flash table (`The word Coney means Counterfeit paper money. / " Bogus " Spurious coin, &c.`) appears on page 113 of Chapter XII of **Jonathan Harrington Green's** *The Secret Band of Brothers; or, A Full and True Exposition of All the Various Crimes, Villanies, and Misdeeds of This Powerful Organization in the United States* (1st book ed., Philadelphia: G. B. Zieber & Co., spring 1847; stereotype reissue, T. B. Peterson, 1858; `CERT-3300-201`, `CERT-3300-203`). Green—born in 1812 and famous in the 1840s as "The Reformed Gambler"—originally serialized Chapters I through X in Cincinnati's *The Casket* between July 1 and September 16, 1846, where editor Emerson Bennett announced that the `"Constitution, By-Laws, Letters, &c."` were being held back for a forthcoming volume (`CERT-3300-202`) while Green himself served as *The Casket*'s publisher (`CERT-3300-214`).

Although Green headed Chapter XII `Hanging Rock, Western District of Virginia, July 12, 1798` (p. 90), five independent internal and external proofs establish that the charter and its flash vocabulary were composed in 1846–1847:

1. **Judicial-district anachronism**: No `"Western District of Virginia"` existed in 1798; Congress divided Virginia west of the Alleghany Mountains twenty-one years later by the *Act of February 4, 1819* (3 Stat. 478, `CERT-3300-205`).
2. **Toponymic anachronisms**: Chapter XII's accompanying cipher correspondence dated `"October 1825"` is datelined from `"Toronto, Canada"` (pp. 115, 125; York, Upper Canada was not incorporated as Toronto until March 6, 1834) and `"Manhattan, Ohio"` (p. 122; not platted at the mouth of the Maumee until 1835).
3. **Explicit editorial disclaimer**: Directly above the page 113 flash table, Green added a bracketed admission on page 112 noting `"there may have been a great many words added to the original vocabulary, since the adoption of the constitution"` (`CERT-3300-201`).
4. **Simultaneous 1847 authorial footnotes**: In his companion Zieber volume *Gambling Unmasked!* (1847, p. 123), Green glossed the exact same pair in his own 1847 prose footnotes (`* Counterfeit paper money, is called "Coney." / † Counterfeit coin, is called "Bogus."`, `CERT-3300-204`).
5. **Diachronic flash controls**: Primary scans of genuine pre-1837 American counterfeiting memoirs, prison exposés, and bank-note detectors—Stephen Burroughs's *Memoirs* (1798, `CERT-3300-206`), Henry Tufts's Dover *"Nomenclature of the Flash Language"* (1807, `CERT-3300-207`), William A. Coffey's *Inside Out* at New York State Prison (1823, `CERT-3300-208`), *Bicknell's Counterfeit Detector* (Feb. 1836, `CERT-3300-210`), and Richard H. Phelps's *Newgate of Connecticut* (1844, `CERT-3300-213`)—yield zero occurrences of *bogus* or clipped *coney*, showing that New York convicts still called Canadian counterfeit bills unclipped `"imported Cogniac"` in 1823 (`CERT-3300-208`) and borderland counterfeiters `"coniackers"` in Lower Canada on October 11, 1836 (`CERT-3300-209`) before *coney* was clipped in the 1840s.

## Pre-Painesville Onomastic Precursor and the 1827–1833 Coining Apparatus

Eliminating the 1847 *Secret Band* phantom restores the May–November 1827 Painesville, Ohio raid as the earliest verified common-noun locus, while surfacing a provocative pre-Painesville proper-name precursor in the Ohio River Valley. On **February 19, 1825**—two years, four months, and seventeen days before the July 6, 1827 *Painesville Telegraph*—T. H. Roberts's *The Microscope, and General Advertiser* (New Albany, Indiana, datelined Frankfort, Kentucky, Feb. 12, 1825) printed a burlesque drama satirizing Kentucky's Relief-Party "New Court of Appeals" and depreciated Bank of the Commonwealth rag paper (`"Lord chief Justass of Rag commerce"`), introducing Attorney General Solomon P. Sharp under the stage name **`Bogus`**: `"Scene 3 --Enter Bogus, as Sharp as a razor, with a sheet of pa- / pe[r] in his hand——Bogus. I pray your honorable selves to hear me"` (`CERT-3300-104`). High-resolution paleographic inspection confirms crisp `B-o-g-u-s` lead type in both roman and italic fonts, while three pre-1827 false friends are eliminated on physical scans: a July 26, 1773 *Boston-Gazette* column-rule bleed misread by OCR as `"Bogus Meeting` (`CERT-3300-101`), an 1822 London satirical speaker tag `HOCUS.` misread as `BOGUS.` (`CERT-3300-102`), and an October 4, 1825 *Pittsburgh Recorder* missionary plural `Bogus` rendering Toba Batak *begu* 'ancestral spirit' (`CERT-3300-103`). Because the 1825 *Microscope* token functions syntactically solely as a proper stage sobriquet alongside `Mungril` and `Tipstaff Payall`, DRAgNEt classifies `CERT-3300-104` as a pre-baseline `onomastic_precursor` rather than a common-noun attestation.

Two years later, in May 1827, authorities in Painesville, Geauga County, Ohio broke up a major gang counterfeiting American half-dollars and Spanish milled dollars (`CERT-3300-105`, `CERT-3300-106`). As *Painesville Telegraph* editor Eber D. Howe recounted in his 1878 *Autobiography and Recollections of a Pioneer Printer* (pp. 34–35), a mysterious two-hundred-pound cast-iron screw and die contraption unearthed during the raid drew a crowd on the Painesville street until `"some one called it a 'Bogus'"` (`CERT-3300-306`). For the next six years along the northern borderland, *bogus* remained a concrete count noun designating the stamping machine itself: when raiders stormed the Cogniac Street counterfeiting sanctuary in Dunham, Lower Canada in August 1833, the inventory published in the *Fayetteville Observer* on September 3, 1833 itemized `"One bogus, or machine impressing dies on the coin, together with a number of dies, engraving tools, bank bill paper, spurious coin, &c."` (`CERT-3300-107`).

## From Machine to Spurious Coin and General Sham (1835–1842)

Metonymic transfer from the cast-iron press to its base-metal coin product—and syntactic reanalysis of `bogus` inside noun-noun compounds (`bogus money`) into an attributive and predicative adjective—occurred along the Lake Erie, Michigan, and Upper Mississippi corridor during the hard-money battles preceding the Panic of 1837:

* **January 17, 1835 (`"Bogus Money"`, -4 years vs. OED/GDoS 1839)**: Reprinting a *Detroit Journal* report of a December 30, 1834 raid on a blacksmith's shop in Brownstown, Wayne County, Michigan, New York's *Working Man's Advocate* reported the seizure of `"a quantity of coin, the particular value of which will be understood if we designate it as 'Bogus Money'"` (`CERT-3300-108`)—advancing *OED* and *Green's* 1839 attributive/adjectival baseline by four calendar years and *Green's* December 7, 1838 counterfeit-coin baseline by three years, ten months, and twenty days.
* **June–December 1837 (Western Reserve & Michigan explosion)**: On June 15, 1837, New Jersey's *Plainfield Union* reprinted `"The Bogus Box"` from Hudson's *Ohio Observer*, detailing a Western Reserve green-goods swindle trading pot-metal for `"Bogus money"` (`CERT-3300-211`, elaborated three weeks later in Baltimore's *The Monument* with `"Bogus men"` and `"his dear 'Bogus'"`, `CERT-3300-110`). In Pontiac, Michigan, Robert McCracken's *Original Miscellaneous Poems* (2nd ed., copyright deposited July 7, 1837, pp. 17–18) printed `'ON BOGUS MONEY'`, uniting the mass noun (`making bogus`), attributive modifier (`Bogus money`), trochaic `/ˈboʊɡəs/` rhyme (`rogue us`), and personal count noun `"He once was thought a GENUINE-- / But now he is a BOGUS"` (`CERT-3300-109`—twenty years ahead of *Green's* 1857 count-noun baseline). By December 1, 1837, Gettysburg's *Star & Republican Banner* reported that `"almost half the specie circulation of Michigan, Illinois and the vicinity is bogus, a term to express spurious coin"` (`CERT-3300-111`).
* **1838–1842 (Unglossed currency triad, compound reversal, and non-monetary sham)**: By May–August 1838, *bogus* circulated unglossed alongside *shinplasters* and *wild-cat* bills from the Mississippi Valley to Kirtland/Tinker's Creek (`CERT-3300-112`, `CERT-3300-113`), followed in 1839 by Caroline M. Kirkland's Michigan novel *A New Home—Who'll Follow?* (calibrated to 1st ed. ch. XXXII, p. 212, `CERT-3300-114`) and rural Maine political press (`CERT-3300-115`). Crucially, by December 21, 1839 (`"the 'bogus machine' was found"` at Niles, Michigan, `CERT-3300-116`, followed by `"Bogus Press"` and `"bogus-making"` in Cincinnati on Aug. 12, 1846, `CERT-3300-214`), speakers had to re-append *machine* or *press* because *bogus* now meant 'counterfeit'. Non-monetary figurative sham application to persons emerged in Albany's *American Masonic Register* on November 20, 1841 (`"a 'bogus' gentleman"`, `CERT-3300-117`—over ten years before *OED*'s 1852 figurative adjective) and October 15, 1842 (`"a bogus Man and a bogus Eagle"`, `CERT-3300-118`), while in June 1842 U.S. Mint assayers Eckfeldt and Du Bois registered `"bogus money"` in official metallurgical literature (`CERT-3300-212`) and London's *Life in the West* (1842) introduced British readers to `"bogus (base coin)"` (`CERT-3300-401`, followed by *Punch* in 1857 and Hotten in 1859, `CERT-3300-405`). Mid-century criminal and geological records further solidified plural `"boguses"` for base coins (Dec. 8, 1849, `CERT-3300-402`), `"bogus gold"` in Chicago's 1850 *Banditti of the Prairies* (`CERT-3300-215`), George W. Matsell's *Vocabulum* (1859/c. 1877, `CERT-3300-216`), Indiana's counterfeiters' stronghold `"Bogus Island"` (1862, `CERT-3300-217`), and Secret Service cant glossaries (1872, `CERT-3300-218`).

## Word-Family Antedatings Across Four Derivatives (1853–1893)

Once general adjectival *bogus* took root in the 1840s, English speakers rapidly derived verbal, occupational, adverbial, and abstract-nominal forms substantially earlier than current historical dictionaries record:

1. **Verbal *bogus, v.* (`bogusing`, Oct. 27, 1853; `bogused`, July 20, 1873)**: Against *Green's Dictionary of Slang*'s 1995 baseline (`bogused`), Savannah's *Daily Morning News* (Oct. 27, 1853, p. 1) warns railway promoters that `"there must be no bogusing in building Railroads"` and mocks `"playing bogus"` (`CERT-3300-403`, a **142-calendar-year** advance), followed by finite preterite `"he bogused and fled"` in the Columbus *Daily Sun* (July 20, 1873) and `"out-bogussing"` in *The Art Amateur* (Oct. 1881, `CERT-3300-408`).
2. **Composing-room printer's *bogus* (1855 vs. OED 1874)**: James Parton's *The Life of Horace Greeley* (1855, pp. 394–395) documents New-York *Tribune* piece-work compositors setting dummy `"Bogus minion"` from the `"bogus-hook"` (`CERT-3300-404`), followed by New York Typographical Union No. 6's 1867 scale rules in Ringwalt's *American Encyclopaedia of Printing* (1871, p. 368: `"When bogus copy is given out in lieu of standing time"`, `CERT-3300-407`)—advancing *OED*'s 1874 printing sense by **19 calendar years** and proving unidirectional specialization from 'sham/dummy' rather than an origin source.
3. **Adverbial *bogusly, adv.* (Jan. 2, 1860 vs. OED July 23, 1862)**: New York's *Hodges' Journal of Finance, and Bank Reporter* (Jan. 2, 1860, p. 2) denounces `"the bogusly organized Tioga County Bank"` (`CERT-3300-406`), advancing *OED* by **2 years, 6 months, and 21 days**.
4. **Abstract nominal *bogusness, n.* (Jan. 25, 1881 & Sept. 4, 1889 vs. OED 1921)**: Speaking in the House of Commons on January 25, 1881, Irish M.P. Tim Healy attacked Land League return statistics as `"a specimen of their 'bogus-ness'"` (*Hansard* Vol. 257, col. 1390, `CERT-3300-409`, a **40-calendar-year** advance over H. L. Mencken's 1921 *OED* baseline), followed in unhyphenated American print by New York's *Musical Courier* on September 4, 1889 (`"genuineness or bogusness"`, p. 215, `CERT-3300-410`). By contrast, systematic sweeps confirm *OED*'s February 26, 1893 *New-York Times* baseline for *bogosity* (corrected from p. 17 to p. 18, `CERT-3300-411`) and expose the sole apparent pre-1917 hit for *bogus caller* as a 1908-for-1982 bound-serial catalog misdate (`CERT-3300-412`).

## Evaluating *Tantrabogus* vs. *Calibogus* and Rebutting Rival Folk Myths

With the 1827 Western Reserve foundry press re-established as the primary common-noun anchor, two pre-existing Northern forms merit serious etymological consideration, while two famous Victorian etymologies collapse under primary-scan scrutiny:

* **Pathway A — New England *tantrabogus* ('ill-looking contraption; bogey')**: Full-book regex inspection of Eber D. Howe's 1878 *Autobiography* (`CERT-3300-306`) reveals an important historiographical correction: contrary to *World Wide Words* and *Grammarphobia*, Howe himself never mentioned *tantrabogus*. Rather, as James A. H. Murray recorded in *OED1* (1887, pp. 967–968, `CERT-3300-307`), Dr. Samuel Willard independently testified that *tantrabogus* had been familiar from his childhood and in his father Julius A. Willard's time in Vermont (pre-1831) for `'any ill-looking object'`—precisely matching a grotesque cast-iron coining press unearthed in heavily Vermont-settled Geauga County, Ohio. Archival scans close the chronological gap that once weakened Willard's report: a Massachusetts soldier's July 2, 1759 Crown Point journal rendered *Ticonderoga* via folk-etymology as `"tantrabogus"` (`CERT-3300-301`), New York's *Mirror* attested bogey-sense `"Mrs. Tabitha Tantrabobus"` beside `"boogaboes"` in 1832 (`CERT-3300-303`), and Samuel Kettell's Boston *Yankee Notions* (1838 [copyright 1837], p. 105) printed `"Tantrabogus"` in triple `/ˌboʊɡəs/` rhyme with `multibrogous`, `cachexagogous`, and `cogue us` (`CERT-3300-305`), alongside West Country cognates `tantarabobs / tantarabobus` ('the devil', Palmer 1837, `CERT-3300-304`) and Devonshire `Tankerabogus` ('a bogie', Hewett 1892, `CERT-3300-308`; contrast the unrelated 1768 Old Bailey surname pun `"Antrobus, who we call Tantrabobus"`, `CERT-3300-302`).
* **Pathway B — Atlantic maritime *calibogus* ('rum and spruce beer') and beverage *bogus* ('rum and molasses')**: North Atlantic *calibogus* is securely attested before 1827 across Sir Joseph Banks's 1766 Newfoundland journal (`"callibogus, egg calli and king calli"`, proving early morphemic segmentation into `calli` + `bogus`, `CERT-3300-309`), George Cartwright's July 4, 1771 Labrador diary and 1792 glossary (`CERT-3300-310`), Francis Grose's 1785 *Classical Dictionary of the Vulgar Tongue* (`CERT-3300-311`), and Royal Navy surgeon Charles Fletcher's 1786 *Maritime State Considered* (`CERT-3300-312`). However, blind inspection of the 1811 *Lexicon Balatronicum* (`CERT-3300-313`) refutes claims of an 1811 two-word `'Cali BOGUS'` print split (the page prints continuous small-caps `CALIBOGUS.`), and simplex beverage `BOGUS` ('A liquor made of rum and molasses', `OED bogus, n.2`) does not appear in print until John Russell Bartlett's 1848 first edition (`CERT-3300-314`, later discussed by Schele De Vere in 1872, `CERT-3300-315`)—twenty-one years after the 1827 Painesville coining press.
* **Falsification of the 1857 "Borghese" and 1867 *bagasse* myths**: Exhaustive searches of 1790–1856 American newspapers confirm zero counterfeiters named *Borghese* (all pre-1857 hits refer to the Roman princely family, `CERT-3300-413`). The eponym myth originated thirty years after Painesville as an anonymous June 12, 1857 *Boston Daily Courier* squib—preserved with its telltale blunder calling *Borghese* `"the Norman name of Borghese"` in the July 1857 *Historical Magazine* (`CERT-3300-414`)—before Ripley and Dana's 1858 *New American Cyclopaedia* silently patched `"Norman"` to `"Italian"`, Bartlett (1859, `CERT-3300-316`) and Mackay (1859, `CERT-3300-415`) canonized it on both sides of the Atlantic, and Schele De Vere (1872, `CERT-3300-417`) subtracted "twenty years ago" from 1857 to invent the precise year `"1837"` (still ten years too late). James Russell Lowell's 1867 *Biglow Papers* conjecture deriving *bogus* from French *bagasse* ('crushed sugar-cane refuse', `CERT-3300-317`) likewise fails French/American oxytone pronunciation (`[ba-ga-s]` / `/bəˈɡæs/`, `CERT-3300-416`), sugar-plantation geography, and coining-press semantics.
