# Doozy: notes toward a documentary history

The lexical question is not simply whether the letters *d-o-o-z-y* appear before 1903. It is whether that earlier writing allows a reader to assign the same sense and grammatical status as the established slang. The *Sunny South* text of June 1, 1901 is secure as ink and date, but the overheard baby talk supplies a strong local motive for reduplication. It should be preserved without assuming either a coinage by Ameringer or borrowing from everyday speech.

Kleberg’s *Slang Fables from Afar* supplies the contrasting ordinary syntactic frame: one Doozy scheme follows another. In a comic account of horse-race betting, “remarkable” or “impressive” describes the schemes, albeit with an ironic context. The typography also capitalizes many ordinary words, so the capital D is not evidence of a proper noun. The Baltimore title and copyright leaves tie the page to the 1903 Phoenix edition. The precise correction to previous transcription is *evolve Schemes*, not *involve Schemes*.

The dialect sources separate lexical meaning from recorded construction. Pound’s 1911 Nebraska list specifies a narrower approving gloss. Warnock’s Part I of 1913 expressly cites it, but her examples give an attributive and a predicative frame. Neither is separately dated in speech. Pound’s 1916 article-plus-*dozy* example is nominal despite its entry label. These sources therefore support a 1903 standalone adjective, a 1911 narrower adjective gloss with additional examples by 1913, and a 1916 count noun. The dates are those of publication, not asserted dates of innovation.

Older slang *daisy* combines approving semantics and productive morphology. Those facts make a relationship plausible, not demonstrable. A clear bridge would require early explicitly related variants or contemporary evidence connecting the terms. The Duse story has no such link. The Model A story has a chronological obstacle; a possible later association is a different hypothesis. A dated and attributable Akeley letter would be a useful next witness for the noun, provided the original wording and context survive.
